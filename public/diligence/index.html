Opportunity diligence

Before I commit to a program, the opportunity gets the same treatment as a design: framed, measured against primary sources, modeled with stated assumptions, and gated. This page is the procedure. It exists because the measurement keeps changing the decision.

Why

Job descriptions, pitch decks, and press releases are marketing surfaces. The record lives somewhere else: federal award ledgers, regulatory filings, corporate registrations, archived job boards, posted compensation bands, disclosure data. The distance between the surface and the record is measurable. Sometimes it is small, and the surface can be trusted. Sometimes it runs ten or twenty to one, and that number is the finding.

Evidence tiers

Every figure in a diligence file carries exactly one tag. The discipline is the product: nothing tagged CLAIMED or MODELED ever gets repeated as fact, by me or by the file.

PRIMARY

A government or corporate record I queried directly: award databases, securities filings, state registrations, archived pages. The strongest tier and the one worth the extra hour.

REPORTED

Journalism or private-market data, carried with its outlet and date. Credible, checkable, and occasionally wrong: a live re-read of a cited article is cheap and has caught misattributions in my own files.

CLAIMED

A company statement with no independent verification. Proof that the claim exists, never proof of its substance. Carried at face value in no model, ever.

MODELED

My own arithmetic from bounded inputs, with every assumption stated and intervals instead of points. Judgment, labeled as judgment.

The procedure

  1. Frame the decision. What is actually being decided, whose call it is, and what evidence would change it. Written first, so the research serves a question instead of wandering.
  2. Sweep the reporting. Both directions: the favorable coverage and the skeptical record, each with dates. Conflicts between sources get kept, not resolved by preference.
  3. Hit the primary sources. The claims-versus-record pass: every load-bearing number the counterparty publishes, measured against the strongest ledger that exists. This step is where decisions actually change.
  4. Model what is unknowable. Burn, runway, compensation, scenarios. Stated assumptions, ranges not points, and a written list of what would falsify each estimate.
  5. Gate the next actions. Cheapest questions first. Nothing binds, and nothing personal moves, until the gate ahead of it passes.
  6. Write the decision record. The ruling, the reasons, the evidence trail, and the falsifiers, dated. Future decisions inherit the file instead of the fog.

The founding case

The method's first full run: a confidential leadership search at a robotics company, August 2026. One evening, three parallel analysis lanes, cross-checked with zero factual conflicts.

The passes: sixteen months of archived job boards, the complete federal prime-award ledger, securities and state filings, compensation receipts from public disclosure data, and a recruiter-channel profile. Headline numbers ran four to twenty times ahead of the primary-source record. The capital base resolved to an interval, not a number. The compensation figure that mattered turned out to be labeled, in small print, an estimate.

The decision closed the same day, in writing, with the reasons and the falsifiers attached. The full case file stays private. The method does not.

What a run produces

  • A decision dossier: the frame, the evidence, the gates, and the dated ruling.
  • A claims-versus-record analysis with every figure tiered and sourced.
  • Financial state and scenario models with stated assumptions and falsifiers.
  • A one-page visual instrument for the decision itself.

The same procedure runs on vendors, partners, acquisitions, and programs. The artifact leads; ask about the method after you have read one.
