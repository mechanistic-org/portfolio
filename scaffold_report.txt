> **Context:** Before the "Factory in a Box" (Makeline), we had to prove we could automate a kitchen without redesigning the building.

The constraint was "Retrofit." We had to add robotic assembly to an existing, chaotic commercial kitchen line without consuming a single inch of extra floor space or disrupting the human chefs working alongside it.

## Engineering Approach

We engineered the "Backsplash"—a shallow-depth linear gantry system designed to mount directly to the rear standard NSF cold rails.

- **Zero-Footprint:** The entire Z-axis and X-axis motion system was compressed into a 6-inch deep volume, effectively replacing the passive stainless steel backsplash of the prep table.
- **Human-Collaborative:** Because it operated directly above human hands, we implemented a "Soft Robotics" safety architecture. The gantries were low-mass and driven by current-limited motors that would E-stop on contact.

## Impact

This was "Gen 1." It proved the physics of automated makelines.

- **Validation:** It successfully assembled test orders at speed, validating the "dispenser-on-gantry" architecture.

## The Challenge
> **Context:** A micromobility fleet needed a way to secure high-value battery packs against theft while allowing authorized "Swap Teams" to replace them in seconds.

The "Halfdome" project was an electromechanical battery lock designed for severe abuse:
*   **Vibration:** It had to stay locked while bouncing down potholes at 20mph.
*   **Security:** It needed to resist pry attacks and picking.
*   **Environment:** It lived outside, exposed to rain, salt spray, and road grime.

## Engineering Approach
We designed a custom motorized locking dog mechanism rather than a simple solenoid (which can vibrate open).

*   **Gearbox Design:** I engineered a high-reduction spur gear train driven by a small DC motor. This provided massive holding torque to the locking pin without requiring continuous power to stay locked (bi-stable design).
*   **Sealing:** The entire mechanism was housed in a glass-filled nylon enclosure with an ultrasonic weld path, achieving IP67 ingress protection.
*   **Feedback:** Integrated Hall Effect sensors provided closed-loop confirmation of "Locked" and "Unlocked" states to the main vehicle controller.

## Impact
This mechanism became the standard retention system for the fleet.
*   **Reliability:** >10,000 cycle life tested under vibration load.
*   **Security:** The non-backdrivable gear train made forced entry nearly impossible without destroying the latch.

M700 / Vesta Project Forensic Report
I. PROJECT SUMMARY
• Role: Lead Mechanical Engineer / "The Architect"
• Mandate: Emergency stabilization of the M700/DV700 Disc Vault fleet. The mandate was to arrest catastrophic field failures—specifically disc jams, scratching, and "unreadable" media errors—driven by mechanical tolerance drift under high-volume usage.
• Core Achievement: Executed a forensic root cause analysis of over 3,000,000 field cycle events to isolate the "Dirty Roller" friction drop, resulting in a complete mechanical subsystem retrofit that eliminated slippage failures and recovered 100% of questioned carousel inventory.
II. THE ANATOMY OF FAILURE (Heuristic Analysis)
Discovery Heuristics applied: Thermal/Mechanical Yield & Quality Control.
• The Trigger (Crisis): The system suffered from a "Friction Coefficient Collapse." High-volume usage revealed that as pinch rollers accumulated environmental dust and grease, their grip degraded, causing discs to stall or eject violently. What are the Dual Radial Spring Balance Bar and pillow block?

Based on the engineering logs and meeting minutes, the Dual Radial Spring Balance Bar and the Pillow Block Extension were two halves of a mechanical redesign implemented to solve the "Dirty Roller" crisis.
They represent a shift in design philosophy from relying on friction (which failed when rollers got dirty) to relying on force (mechanical clamping), and the structural reinforcement required to handle that extra force.
1. The Dual Radial Spring Balance Bar
This was a redesigned spring mechanism for the pinch roller assembly.
• Purpose: To apply significantly higher clamping force to the pinch rollers. The original "grippy" coating on the rollers failed in the field because dust and grease accumulated on them, causing a drop in friction and allowing discs to slip.
• Function: By increasing the spring force (up to 2x or 3x the original specification), the system could forcefully grab and move discs even when the rollers were slippery with dust, effectively ignoring the coefficient of friction.
• Design: It replaced the original leaf spring design with a more robust "balance bar" configuration to distribute this increased load evenly across the rollers.
2. The Pillow Block (and Extension)
The pillow blocks (Upper and Lower) are the plastic bearing housings that hold the steel shafts of the pinch rollers.
• The Problem (Side Effect): The new, stronger springs (Dual Radial Spring Balance Bar) worked too well. They applied so much force that they caused the steel roller shafts to bend (deflect). This deflection caused the rollers to misalign with the Optical Disc Drive (ODD), resulting in discs jamming or crashing into the slot ribs during ejection.
• The Fix (The Extension): To stop the shafts from bending, Erik Norris designed a Pillow Block Extension. This modified the lower pillow block to extend further down the shaft, providing a rigid support point closer to the gear and motor. This "stiffened" the driven roller, preventing the deflection caused by the high-tension springs and ensuring discs traveled in a straight line.
Summary of the Engineering Loop:
1. Failure: Rollers get dirty -> Discs slip.
2. Intervention 1: Install Dual Radial Spring Balance Bar (Stronger Springs) to grab dirty discs.
3. New Failure: Stronger springs bend the roller shafts -> Discs jam.
4. Intervention 2: Design Pillow Block Extension to support the shafts against the new spring load.

• The Path: Instead of traveling in a straight line back into the carousel slot, the disc would veer clockwise (relative to the carousel center).
• The Crash: This angled trajectory caused the leading edge of the disc to miss the slot opening and crash head-on into the slot rib (specifically around slot 26 in testing).
3. The Structural Fix (Pillow Block Extension)
To eliminate this bias, Erik Norris designed a modified Inner Disc Guide and Bearing Block.
• Support Wing: This new part included a "pillow block extension" that physically filled the void where the shaft was bending.
• Rigid Backstop: It provided a hard plastic support point closer to the center of the shaft's span, preventing the deflection even under "double force" spring tension.
• Result: This restored the straight-line trajectory, allowing the stronger springs to be used without causing the discs to crash into the carousel ribs.
How did the 'sister ribs' tool change stabilize the carousel?

Based on the forensic engineering logs and tooling quotations, the addition of 320 "Sister Ribs" was a critical "Hard Tooling Retrofit" designed to stabilize the carousel by solving the Injection Pathology (the conflict between Flash and Short Shots).
Here is the technical breakdown of how this tool change stabilized the component:
1. Opening the "Process Window"
The primary instability of the carousel was the lack of a viable molding process window. The 320 original ribs were too thin and long for the plastic to flow through easily.
• Before (The Trap): To fill the original ribs, the molder had to use "intentional over-pressure," which blew open the mold parting lines and caused "massive flash" in the disc slots. If they lowered the pressure to stop the flash, the plastic froze before reaching the end, causing "short shots".
• After (The Fix): The Sister Ribs acted as "Flow Leaders" or auxiliary channels. They effectively doubled the cross-sectional area available for plastic flow. This allowed the resin to flow to the outer diameter of the 390mm part at lower pressures, filling the mold completely without forcing the tool open and creating flash.
2. Eliminating "Flow Hesitation"
The design required plastic to flow from a thick inner ring to thin outer ribs—a transition described as "crossing the moat".
• Thermal Stabilization: In the original tool, the plastic would hesitate at this transition and cool down, leading to internal stress and brittleness. The sister ribs provided a continuous thermal path, keeping the flow front hot and moving.
• Structural Homogeneity: By ensuring even filling, the sister ribs reduced the internal material stress that contributed to the "Potato Chip" warping effect, where carousels would distort by 3-4mm after cooling.
3. Structural Reinforcement
The addition of the ribs mechanically stiffened the carousel structure.
• Deflection Control: The added material increased the vertical rigidity of the carousel, helping to resist the "whipping" or vertical deflection that could occur during rotation or shock events.
• Cost/Logistics: The retrofit cost approximately $12,000 and required a 4-week lead time. It was a massive intervention, effectively re-cutting the entire steel cavity to save the project from low yields.

Plastic cups are stacked tightly. Static friction and vacuum lock make them stick together. If you grab one and get two, the machine jams. If you grab zero, you dump ranch dressing onto the conveyor belt.

## Engineering Approach
We architected a standalone electromechanical module that could separate, fill, and stage cups for the main line.

*   **Peristaltic Dosing:** To handle viscous fluids (Salsa, Ranch, Hummus) without cross-contamination, we utilized a peristaltic pump loop. This meant the machine never touched the food—only the FDA-approved tubing did.
