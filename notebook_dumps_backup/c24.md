# C|24 ("Curtis") Forensic Report

**Source:** NotebookLM

**Subject:** C|24 Console (Codename: Curtis)

## I. PROJECT SUMMARY

- **Role:** Mechanical Engineering Lead / Industrial Design Lead
- **Mandate:** Execute a total platform modernization of the legacy Control|24 console. Objectives: Achieve RoHS compliance, integrate 5.1 surround monitoring, eliminate the Focusrite licensing royalty, and maintain a $9,995 MSRP while improving manufacturability.
- **Core Achievement:** Solo-architected the mechanical integration of 19 distinct Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) into a low-profile chassis and engineered a proprietary "Vertical Hanging" manufacturing process to salvage the cosmetic yield of the product's primary visual elements during a critical Pilot line-down crisis.

## II. THE ANATOMY OF FAILURE (Heuristic Analysis)

### 1. The "Banana" Defect (Thermal/Mechanical Crisis)

- **The Trigger:** During the Pilot build (June-July 2007), the ABS "Side Cap" components (P/N 9440-55165/166) consistently failed incoming inspection. Parts exhibited severe bowing and twisting, deviating up to **2.50mm** from flatness specifications, creating unacceptable gaps against the sheet metal chassis.
- **The Failure:** Forensic analysis of inspection data (`before_and_after_rubber_paint.pdf`) revealed that the "Rubberized Soft Paint" curing process—requiring high-temperature baking—was causing the ABS substrate to shrink linearly by **\~2.27mm** (from 756.51mm to 754.24mm) and sag due to improper racking. The vendor's standard "flat rack" method (Method C) was thermally deforming the parts beyond recovery.
- **The Fix:** I rejected the vendor's (Jetcrown) proposed "support block" workarounds. I engineered and enforced **"Method A"**, a **Vertical Hanging Fixture** protocol. By suspending the parts vertically during the cure cycle, I utilized gravity to maintain part straightness during the glass-transition phase of the plastic. This process change was codified in **ECO 12740**, reducing flatness deviation to **\<0.50mm** and saving the Pilot schedule.

### 2. The Top Panel "No-Bid" (Supply Chain Fracture)

- **The Trigger:** The primary overseas contract manufacturer (VTech/Kwanta) issued a "no-bid" on the product's main interface surface, the Top Panel (9420-55105-00), citing extreme complexity.
- **The Failure:** The design required a high density of welded standoffs (over 30 studs on a single panel) to support the channel strip PCBs. The vendor claimed their automated stud-welding equipment could not handle the proximity of the hardware, threatening to stall the entire production line.
- **The Fix:** I executed a rapid **Dual-Source Bridge Strategy**. I leveraged domestic partner **Mass Precision** (Silicon Valley) to fabricate emergency sheet metal sets to keep the Pilot build alive (Source: `RE_ DigiDelivery from Erik Norris_ _Preliminary Curtis sheetmetal...`). Simultaneously, I negotiated a manual offset-welding process with the overseas vendor to bridge production until their automation could be retooled, preventing a "Stop Ship" scenario.

### 3. The Headphone Jack Fire Drill (Serviceability)

- **The Trigger:** Late in the design phase (April 2007), Customer Service data revealed the headphone jack on the legacy unit had a **4.8% field failure rate** (Source: `RE_ Potential Field_CS concern...`).
- **The Failure:** The original C|24 mechanical design buried this jack beneath the fader banks, requiring a 2+ hour teardown for a common repair.
- **The Fix:** I executed an emergency redesign of the Sheet Metal Headphone Bracket (9420-55126-00) and Plastic Front Bolster (9440-55167-00). I created a recessed "trap door" geometry, allowing the jack to be serviced from the bottom-front by removing a single nut and the bottom pan, drastically reducing Mean Time To Repair (MTTR).

## IV. TACTICAL ENGINEERING (Deep Dives)

### 1. Manual Offset Welding (The "Bridge" Tactic)

To bypass the "No-Bid" block on the Top Panel, I authorized a specific deviation from standard automated manufacturing:

- **The Constraint:** Standard automated CNC stud-welding heads are bulky. The C|24 design placed studs so close together that the machine head physically could not fit between them without colliding with neighbor studs.
- **The Workaround:** I authorized the use of a **"manual offset welder."** This tool features a modified tip geometry where the electrode is offset from the gun body, allowing a human operator to reach into tight "canyons" between existing studs.
- **The Execution:**
  - **Domestic (Mass Precision):** Ed Stegall used this to fabricate the initial "freebies for a fit check," unblocking the Pilot build.
  - **Overseas (Kenny):** I negotiated with VTech to adopt this manual process for the initial 500 units, securing Q4 revenue while they engineered custom fixtures for long-term automation.
- **The Cost:** This manual intervention required intense quality management, as early units exhibited "ripples and dents" from handling, requiring me to enforce stricter cosmetic standards on the manual line.

### 2. Bourns Jog Wheel Integration (Value Engineering)

In January 2007 (10 months pre-launch), we faced a cost crisis. The legacy jog wheel was too expensive for the new margin targets.

- **The Component:** We selected the **Bourns EM14** (14mm Rotary Optical Encoder), a standard component significantly smaller than the legacy custom mechanism.
- **The Integration:** I engineered a **custom plastic surround** to adapt this tiny encoder to the large console chassis. This required precise tolerance analysis to ensure the heavy knob felt "premium" and stable despite being mounted on a commodity shaft.
- **The Save:** During First Article inspection, I caught a critical defect where the sample units arrived with **"six exposed round wire leads"** instead of a connector. I calipered the leads (~4.75mm high) and enforced a specification for a pre-terminated harness to prevent a high-risk manual soldering operation on the production line.

### 3. The DCD Protocol (Geometric Firewall)

The DCD (Data Control Drawing) was not just paper; it was a weapon against entropy.

- **The Mechanism:** I refused to accept any PCB layout that did not conform to the released DCD. I required layout designers to submit **DXF files** of their placements, which I imported back into the Pro/Engineer Master Assembly for interference checks.
- **Confirmed Interceptions:**
  - **SubMix I/O Dead Zone:** I rejected a layout where connectors were placed in a "dead zone" inaccessible to assembly workers. I forced a move to the board edge (DCD Rev 5).
  - **Time Code Display:** My 3D check revealed LED displays were floating **8.12mm too high**, destined to crash into the metalwork. I forced a layout change (DCD Rev 6).
  - **Select C Short:** Caught a mounting hole overlapping a component pad in the DXF, preventing a guaranteed electrical short.
- **The Result:** 100% mechanical fit on the first physical build of the Pilot units.
