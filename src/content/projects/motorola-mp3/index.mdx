## A phone accessory built around a fixed envelope

Motorola's MP3 accessory put music controls and removable memory between a compatible phone and a headset. The development brief called for a wearable, tie-clip unit powered through the phone connection, with an SD/MMC card and a USB connection for transferring music from a computer. The enclosure had to bring those interfaces together within an unusually small exterior. {/* ^[evidence:LK-dcd2291a] ^[evidence:LK-934ab7bc] */}

Through Mechanistic, I developed the 3D mechanical layout and internal architecture, along with the exterior surfacing and contouring. I worked from sketches and preliminary surfaces, built the master model, and broke it into individual parts. That included modeling both custom parts and off-the-shelf components through several review and comment cycles. My role also covered industrial-design liaison, machined check-model iterations and manufacturing support for Solteras, which was developing the accessory for Motorola. {/* ^[evidence:EV-1ecbc8a5ef8b] ^[evidence:LK-dcd2291a] */}

## From surfaces to individual parts

The external form was a constraint from the beginning. Curved housing surfaces, contrasting soft-touch areas and the transitions between housing pieces had to coexist with the internal assembly. The mechanical task was to preserve that form while giving the boards, memory card, audio connections and controls enough space to fit and operate. {/* ^[evidence:LK-934ab7bc] ^[evidence:EV-1ecbc8a5ef8b] */}

The master model connected the exterior surfaces to the internal layout before the geometry was broken into individual parts. Developing the custom components alongside models of the off-the-shelf parts made their interfaces part of the same design process. The project retrospective records localized wall thinning where components needed clearance, followed by several machined check models and design reviews. Those physical checks were part of developing the assembly before the detailed CAD was delivered overseas. {/* ^[evidence:EV-1ecbc8a5ef8b] ^[evidence:LK-934ab7bc] */}

## Making the interfaces explicit

The review exchanges show how much of the work sat between components. The clip attachment and memory-card connection competed for room. The board-to-board connector, switch housing and second PCB required defined clearances. Around the audio jack, the housing needed to support the connector while preserving the surrounding wall and assembly geometry. These were specific packaging questions to work through with the electrical and manufacturing contributors. {/* ^[evidence:LK-c98cc229] ^[evidence:LK-138a1b37] */}

The October 19 review asked for Pro/ENGINEER sections showing the minijack's position relative to the housing and clarifying its support. It also asked for explanations of proposed rib, switch and local geometry changes. Section views made the questions concrete: where a part sat, what supported it, and which clearance or tooling condition still needed a decision. {/* ^[evidence:LK-424058db] */}

My October 30 package continued that discussion with sections through the cord, buttons, clip and logo lens. The button sections asked for the desired spring-finger design and microswitch specifications; another view asked for the clearance between the switch housing and PCB2. The logo-lens review separated the visible lens from questions about the light-pipe path, LED position and transparent area. These annotations recorded the interfaces still requiring agreement, alongside the developed geometry. {/* ^[evidence:LK-2f227431] */}

## Leaving room for manufacturing decisions

Clearance and manufacturability had to be considered together. Local wall thinning could recover internal space, while the manufacturer could still need more material or a different tooling condition elsewhere. A supplier comment relayed on October 31 raised a temperature-shock deformation concern and requested as much thickness as possible. It records a concern and a requested change; the available correspondence does not establish a completed thermal qualification. {/* ^[evidence:LK-934ab7bc] ^[evidence:LK-f0684949] */}

The cord section provides a more specific example of the design's state. My October 30 annotation records that the cord area had grown to accommodate an increased cord diameter, while asking for confirmation of strain-relief wall thickness, use of a metal crimp, and rib diameter and spacing. A reported geometry change and open supplier decisions appear on the same sheet. {/* ^[evidence:LK-2f227431] */}

## Delivering the model and its remaining questions

On October 30, 2000, I delivered the revised assembly and recorded completion of Phase 2. My cover note to Julie Ma described the remaining issues as local tuning associated with tooling, and proposed that Shinwoo assume ownership of the assembly for further manufacturing refinement. I emphasized that the model could accommodate changes to the elastomer wall thickness and other tooling details. {/* ^[evidence:LK-2f227431] */}

That was a defined CAD deliverable, with an explicit account of what still needed attention. A November 3 fax from Motorola's Venki Sampath to Julie and me carried further concerns and suggestions. The handoff and subsequent review belong to the same development history; delivery of the model did not mean every supplier decision or test was complete. {/* ^[evidence:LK-2f227431] ^[evidence:LK-b8a78a2b] */}

The dated record places this mechanical work in 2000: a July 27 assembly drawing, September main- and switch-PCB documents using the Typan name, and the October delivery and review exchanges. My contribution was the architecture and geometry that connected the product's external form to its internal parts, developed through check models and the detailed work of communicating with the people who would manufacture it. {/* ^[evidence:LK-f1f00f64] ^[evidence:LK-1abe4c9c] ^[evidence:LK-2f227431] ^[evidence:EV-1ecbc8a5ef8b] */}
