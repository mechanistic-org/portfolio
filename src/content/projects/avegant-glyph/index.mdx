## Joining Avegant

[Richard Lane](https://www.linkedin.com/in/richard-lane-5525751), my former boss at Kaleidescape, recommended me to [Joerg Tewes](https://www.linkedin.com/in/joergtewes/), Avegant's CEO. I joined on July 20, 2015, as a Senior Mechanical Engineer to help get Glyph into production and get its overdue Kickstarter deliveries moving. Glyph was the main reason I was hired and became the bulk of my work there.

Glyph combined headphones and a personal display in a 434 g (15.3 oz) headset.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */} Lower the band across your eyes and it became a display; raise it and it returned to headphone use. I took responsibility for the whole mechanical bill of materials: getting the plastic and metal parts, springs, cables, pads and their interfaces through tooling validation and production ramp. Color, material, finish, print and graphics were part of that responsibility too.

### An overdue Kickstarter commitment

Before I joined, I looked into the overdue Kickstarter deliveries and the growing backer frustration. The product design was established, but there was still a lot of work between that design and reliable production. I was coming in to help close that gap.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

The campaign had raised $1,509,506 from 3,331 backers. The original hardware reward was a beta headset, estimated for December 2014; Avegant subsequently committed to delivering a consumer product instead. When I arrived, that original delivery estimate was already seven months past.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

I worked on as many fronts as I could at once. Assembly and fit, cable binding, dust contamination, liner behavior and comfort could all hold up shipment. Pilots and production builds exposed some problems; testing, people wearing the product and customer feedback exposed others. A change to one part had to work within the rest of the assembly, be practical for the supplier to make, and go through the validation it needed. That work continued as production ramped and deliveries began.

It was pandemonium. Testing, supplier questions, tooling changes and production problems were all moving at once. I had a lot of plates spinning, moving between the immediate build problems and the changes needed for the next round.{/* ^[evidence:EV-ac59855ac970] */}

## Fitting two optical engines into the headband

Inside the band were two miniature DLP optical engines, one for each eye. Each used LEDs to illuminate a Texas Instruments digital micromirror device: a 1280 × 720 array of tilting mirrors on a 0.3-inch diagonal. With a mirror pitch of 5.4 µm, the active array occupied just 6.912 × 3.888 mm. Together, the two eyes had about 1.84 million mirrors forming their images.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

The mirrors switched light into or away from the viewing path. Custom optics delivered that light through the eyepiece and into the pupil, where the eye's own lens focused it onto the retina. Avegant called this Retinal Imaging Technology. The wearer viewed the illuminated micromirror image through the optics, with an individual focus adjustment for each eye.

For our mechanical work, the challenge extended from the optical assemblies to the person wearing them. Within each engine, the DMD, illumination optics and eyepiece had to maintain their geometry. The two engines also had to stay aligned with each other as the wearer adjusted their spacing, or interpupillary distance (IPD). The chassis supported those relationships inside a headset that flexed, telescoped and moved between headphone and display positions.

Even measuring the assembly required care. The alignment-fixture specification called for three mounting locations that would not force the chassis flat, and checks at both minimum and maximum IPD. Clamping a chassis into a different shape could conceal the geometry we needed to measure.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */} Nose height, headband position and the separate focus controls then let the wearer bring both images into view. Mechanical fit and optical performance met at those interfaces.

## Getting the mechanical BOM ready to build

<span id="shared-glyph-production" class="block scroll-mt-24"></span>

My tooling work ran from design through ramp.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */} I worked with [Chris Westra](https://www.linkedin.com/in/mtuchris/), [Misha Young](https://www.linkedin.com/in/logicdw), our contract manufacturer Intretech and the component suppliers to turn tooling samples into parts we could build with. That involved inspecting dimensions and appearance, checking how the parts fitted together, getting tool changes made and deciding what could proceed into a build.

Keeping a build moving meant knowing which parts were ready and what still stood between us and a complete set. In late August 2015, we were working toward material readiness for 10 sets before August 26 and 135 sets before September 3. The headband BOM tied those quantities to responsible people, first article inspections, review status, whether a part was usable for engineering validation, and the actions needed from the supplier. A part could be usable for that build while still needing a tooling correction for production.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}



<div data-authoring-group="tool-trials"></div>

### The outer-headband tool

Of the roughly 40 tools I was responsible for on Glyph, the outer-headband tool was the trickiest. The part, 425-0003, was a sweeping arch nearly 200 mm across, molded in SABIC EXL1414 impact-modified polycarbonate.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

The arch also carried small tabs, clips and screw features. The revision-8 drawing called for 15 M1.6 brass inserts installed ultrasonically, and different molding finishes across its surfaces, including SPI-A1-polished areas where gates and parting lines were prohibited.



<div data-authoring-group="figure-3"></div>

<div data-authoring-group="headband-mold-flow"></div>

The trial parts also showed cracks at the knit-line locations visible in the simulation.{/* ^[evidence:EV-e58d30973414] */}

<div data-authoring-group="knit-line-cracks"></div>



The part geometry, internal features, ejection method and practical tooling constraints all forced compromises on one another. Clearing the undercuts required a collapsing internal core and opposing slides on each side. When the mechanism worked correctly, the part cleared the tool without needing to deform. Getting the tool actions, ejection and cooling to work repeatably was difficult.{/* ^[evidence:EV-e58d30973414] */}



<div data-authoring-group="figure-4"></div>



In one failure mode, ejection pressure against the sticking part broke it. Getting a part out intact was not enough either: we also had trials with correct ejection while the part was still dimensionally unstable.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}



<span id="media-figure-6"></span>
<div data-authoring-group="figure-5"></div>









We tried various post-molding cooling fixtures, but they weren't a viable route to higher-volume production. I had to define and control the molding process so the part could leave the tool in a controlled condition and cool into the shape and dimensions we needed.

Moving to a hot-runner setup was a big part of getting the outer-headband molding to work.{/* ^[evidence:EV-51df7190f9f8] */} I ended up tuning the process parameters to produce the right as-molded part weight. That gave us parts that could eject and then cool into the right shape and size, repeatably. This kind of process tuning is familiar work in plastic molding. What made the headband difficult was how sensitive the whole part and tooling system was.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

How we held the part for inspection mattered, as did what happened when it met the surrounding parts. Hi-P, a supplier reviewing tooling samples, flagged distortion, difficulty establishing a measurement datum and flash at local features. Working through those findings meant deciding what needed a better measurement, what needed an assembly check and what needed a change to the part or tool.

Our August 26, 2015 engineering review listed 16 discrepancies. The overall span measured 197.06 mm against a nominal 196.31 mm. The inspection sheet flagged it against the drawing tolerance; the engineering review accepted it as a reference dimension. That disposition applied to that dimension, while the other findings still needed their own decisions.

A local feature that might have warped needed to be measured in a fixture. At a mating interface, the review called for checking that assembly did not deform the parts. At the screw-capture ledge, the problem was more specific: the screw was bottoming out. The ledge measured 1.133–1.140 mm, and the review called for raising it to at least 1.15 mm.



<div data-authoring-group="figure-7"></div>



The outer headband continued to change after shipping began. Its drawing package reached revision 25 in May 2016. The later revision history records detent and paint-thickness changes, a boss-counterbore change, and revisions to the artwork and its placement. The finish drawings mapped soft-touch black paint and black UV hard coat to different regions, with explicit masking and overspray limits. Fit, finish and appearance kept meeting in this same part.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

### Keeping the wider build moving

I delegated as much of the drawing and documentation work as I could. [Leonard Pang](https://www.linkedin.com/in/leonard-pang-bb888b127) and others helped with drawings and revisions. That let me protect time for troubleshooting, supplier work and the decisions needed across the mechanical BOM.{/* ^[evidence:EV-793368ad06ec] */}

That work extended across the BOM: spring indexing, gimbal holes and pins, sliding and bearing surfaces, snaps, screw features, texture and finish. We had to distinguish a drawing or measurement problem from a tooling or processing problem, agree on the correction and check the next parts. Chris, Misha, the supplier engineers and I worked through those reviews together.

The first question with a fix was how much validation it would take. Then came what the supply chain and fabrication could support. I coordinated on the fly, trying to make the best use of time and money. I worked for Chris Westra and [Allan Evans](https://www.linkedin.com/in/allan-evans-phd/), who approved major expenditures. Those practical limits shaped how much we could change, and when.

I've always been an avid photographer, and I use photography to communicate technical work. At Avegant, photographing parts, assemblies and the work around them was part of pursuing good parts at volume and acceptable yield.

## Defining and running qualification

I worked with Intretech on how we would qualify the product and validate changes. The work covered the assembled headset's moving interfaces, environmental exposure, drops, buttons and connectors, as well as the durability of coatings, printing and packaging. It involved test samples and fixtures, operating conditions, inspections during testing and functional checks afterward.

### Exercising the assembly

The fixtures had to reproduce the movement we cared about: opening the headband, extending and returning the arms, and moving the earcup interfaces. Travel, speed, applied force and the position of the stops all mattered. In September 2015, I requested an adjustment to the headband-life fixture so it could exercise the assembly at full extension.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

These three short clips show a few of the fixtures exercising Glyph. They make the motion visible; the measurements, inspections and functional checks tell us what happened to the product.

<div data-authoring-group="qualification-fixtures"></div>

For the headband, we specified 3,000 cycles, checking every 500 and measuring the opening force before and after. The telescoping arms also had a 3,000-cycle procedure. Environmental exposure, drops and finish tests addressed other ways the assembly could change or stop working.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

### Checking function while the interface moved

The micro-HDMI investigation shows why a functional check at rest was only part of the test. In August 2015, the connector was repeatedly inserted, removed and loaded in four directions, with dimensional and free-play measurements before and after. The display could look normal at a checkpoint, then briefly turn green or black during the separate wiggle check before recovering. Those observations made the behavior under movement visible alongside the changes in the physical interface.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

### Following what the tests exposed

The March production-test records contain passing life and finish tests alongside failures that needed investigation. Vibration exposed a lock spring coming loose. Eyepiece cycling exposed a failure to lock, which the report attributed to an overly tight screw. Environmental testing also recorded display and audio problems. These were specific observations on tested assemblies; they did not all point to the same cause or correction.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

<span id="shared-glyph-changes" class="block scroll-mt-24"></span>

We planned the September reliability allocation around a pool of 20 units, assigning samples across the different tests. As the design and process changed, specific fixes needed their own validation. In April 2016, after initial shipping had begun, I had cable-abrasion fixes in validation on 20 units for headband lifecycle testing and another 10 for drop testing. Short-term chassis machining, cable rework and a revised routing scheme were being evaluated alongside longer-term tooling and harness changes.

<div data-authoring-group="figure-8"></div>

Qualification continued through the ramp. I had to coordinate the parts and sample configurations, the supplier changes and the testing needed to put a change into production, while backer deliveries were still underway. Comfort brought us back to people wearing the assemblies we were making.

## Making Glyph wearable

### Taking the load off the nose

I couldn't wear Glyph for more than a few minutes without pain. We kept hearing that people's ears hurt, and the silicone nosepads left deep, angry marks on many test subjects that took a long time to disappear.

<span id="shared-glyph-headstrap" class="block scroll-mt-24"></span>

Radius Product Development supported the headstrap work.

The headstrap was a late addition. I proposed it, got it approved, tested it and brought it into production relatively quickly. Radius developed support concepts and mockups during that work. I added receiver features to the earcans for quick-release buckles, so the strap could be removed and Glyph could still be used without it.

With the strap attached to both earcans and adjusted over the crown, it took almost all the load off the nosepiece. The nosepiece then mainly located the unit, while the top of the head carried the weight. The strap also added another contact that helped keep the display in place.

It was a hold-your-nose solution. The strap was big and bulky, caught in people's hair and was tricky to adjust correctly the first time. It basically turned the headphones into a hat. We accepted it reluctantly, late in a long and frustrating engineering effort under pressure to deliver the overdue Kickstarter product. Once people got the hang of it, though, it became essential for longer use. It was the comfort fix I felt had the most impact.

<span id="shared-glyph-fit" class="block scroll-mt-24"></span>

Keeping it optional left another constraint in place: the earcups still had to grip hard enough to hold Glyph without the strap. I reduced the clamp as far as I could while preserving that use. Even then, it was too much for me to wear comfortably for long, with or without the strap. Taking weight off the nose made a substantial difference; the ear pressure remained.

#### Tuning the pivot feel

I also specified a damping grease for the headstrap pivot to get the feel and resistance right. I wanted something like the controlled movement of an expensive volume knob. Pivot torque testing was part of that work.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

### Measuring the assembled clamp force

Clamp force changed with both head width and telescope position. We measured the assembled headset across those settings while comparing spring and liner configurations. Jill helped with assembly and testing during the spring-and-liner work.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */} At 150 mm width and 20 mm extension, the recorded 1.6, 1.4 and 1.2 mm spring groups averaged 11.7, 11.0 and 9.6 N. The curves show why a single force number could not describe the fit.{/* ^[evidence:EV-17ab19c5ec5f] */}

<div data-authoring-group="clamp-response"></div>

My recollection is that we settled on 1.4 mm springs with 60A inner liners.{/* ^[evidence:EV-51df7190f9f8] */}

### Checking the parts behind the force curves

The springs needed their own dimensional checks. In May 2016, an inspection compared ten 1.4 mm headband springs from each of two supplier lots. On one 148.30 ± 2.00 mm feature, the recorded means were 145.605 mm and 145.766 mm; five of ten parts in the first lot and eight of ten in the second were outside the drawing limits. The second lot had less scatter, but its tighter grouping was still offset from the required dimension. Consistency and conformance were different questions.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

Those part measurements complemented the assembled-headset tests. A spring drawing, an inspection result and a force curve each described a different part of the problem: the shape we requested, the parts we received and what the complete assembly did.

### Developing the earpads

The earpads were a development effort in their own right, balancing contact pressure, comfort, traction and appearance.

I tore down numerous headphones, obtained vendor samples and bought replacement earpads for a wide range of models. These gave me a broad set of constructions to examine while designing and prototyping Glyph's pads. My favorite Bose earpads were a reference for the foam type and combination.

We also compared how pad surfaces gripped an adjustable acrylic fixture. At a 132 mm span, we added weight to a hanging bag until the headset slipped. The seven variants behaved very differently; four examples show the range:

| Pad surface | Recorded hanging mass at slip |
| --- | ---: |
| Normal Vulcan dots | 656.9 g |
| Silicone netting | 1,081.74 g |
| Gel dots | 1,000.04 g |
| Suede | 35.98 g |

The suede result included the impact of the first bearing landing in the bag. At a wider 155 mm setting, the normal dotted pads held beyond 1,637.3 g before we ran out of weights. The fixture gave us a comparison under a common setup, but acrylic could produce more contact and suction than a person's head. These were bench retention measurements; acoustic seal and comfort still depended on people wearing the product.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

I built up a group of Glyphs with the different earpad variants and had colleagues try them and provide comments. Nearly everyone liked the dots and the low-pressure foam, and I used that feedback to make the selection. We chose the normal dotted surface, shown in the finished earpad photograph, rather than the gel dots or silicone netting.{/* ^[evidence:EV-51df7190f9f8] */}

### The inner liner and its fixed constraints

The inner liners were among the most difficult parts I worked on. They were two-shot molded parts, but the tooling itself was straightforward enough. The harder problem was getting the part to satisfy its functions and interfaces throughout the assembly’s movement.

During flex or twist, the liner and its mating flange did not deform together. A small gap would open at the edge, like a coin purse: the dreaded pucker.

The surrounding parts were largely off-limits, apart from subtle, steel-safe changes. Travel and extension distance were fixed. The liner had to work within those interfaces and motions.

I was trying to connect the failures I saw during cycle testing and use with how the materials deformed together in the assembly. I tried incremental changes during tool fabrication and trials, but that region never worked correctly through every aspect and axis of its travel.

In practice, we had to live with some puckering and deformation. A longer production run at higher volume might have supported further development. I wasn’t prepared to call the problem impossible, but I didn’t believe that liner, as it stood, could satisfy every constraint.

The product had to ship with those compromises still in place.

## Movement, reliability and dust

### Cable routing through a moving assembly

The cable could work initially, then develop a kink after repeated cycling and bind soon afterward. Adding stiffness could help it resist buckling, but the bundle still had to fit and move through a confined path. Protective tape could curl at its edges and increase the effort needed to telescope the arm. Local stiffness, bundle size, friction and routing had to work together.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

I spent a great deal of time working through those combinations: splints, different jackets and wires, changes to the looming specifications. Careful design of experiments, lots of changes and lots of cycles.

The slack for telescoping extension lived inside the earcan, wound around the center as a service loop. Adjacent features guided and housed it as it expanded and contracted. The pass-throughs at each end of the telescoping sections mattered too. The pivot arm could bind the cable against the housing in several positions, and long, curved surfaces sliding past one another offered more places to snag. Fixing points, routing and variation in the cable and its covering all affected how it behaved.



<div data-authoring-group="cable-cycling"></div>



Cycle testing exposed kinking and abrasion across multiple harness variants. The photographs record the different ways the cables were damaged as the earcans were extended and retracted.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

The comparative cable builds used identified assemblies and staged inspections. The protocol called for opening the units, photographing the cable in its collapsed position and reassembling them between checkpoints. That let us examine how a construction changed as it accumulated cycles, as well as whether the arm still returned.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

An idea also had to survive fabrication and assembly. Fujikura withdrew one PET-film stiffening proposal because the film peeled open before or during the spiral-wrapping operation. Other discussions compared sidewalls, splints, wrap widths and wrap direction. A promising cross-section was only one part of making a workable harness.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}



<span id="shared-glyph-cohorts" class="block scroll-mt-24"></span>

Two five-unit groups tested in March 2016 give a sense of the range of behavior. In T6, two units could not return at 250 cycles. The separate T5 group recorded OK wear and kink checks through 3,000 cycles.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}

The April weekly updates record the six-variant comparison as complete and the splint approach as rejected. Short-term cable rework and chassis machining were moving alongside longer-term tooling changes and samples from a second supplier. Decisions were being made and changes released while other investigations continued.{/* ^[evidence:EV-caf0e7c1af31] */}

The near-term and longer-term work ran together:

| Workstream | What we were changing |
| --- | --- |
| Existing production assemblies | Chassis machining, cable rework, protective tape and routing |
| Production tooling and harness design | Changes intended to replace repeated secondary operations and rework |
| Alternative cable construction | Amphenol tube/sleeve concepts and samples, while Fujikura sustaining work continued |

Mechanical ECOs for the surrounding parts were released while the alternative cable samples remained a separate development effort. The practical task was to keep improving the assembly in production while evaluating what should replace it.{/* ^[evidence:EV-caf0e7c1af31] */}


The work carried into production through careful harness assembly, inspection and system assembly. During the short production run, I instituted a mid-build "break-in" cycling station to test and validate the process and support ongoing improvement. I specified cycling 20% of units; in practice, we only did one in ten. No kink meant pass and continue through assembly.

#### Electrical checks and physical condition

Electrical continuity did not tell the whole story. Of 17 cables returned from the production line for supplier analysis, 11 passed the electrical retest, five had open or short circuits, and one connector was too damaged to test. Some of the electrically passing cables still showed visible jacket or connector damage. Inspecting the physical condition answered a different question from checking whether the wires still conducted.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

The work extended into retention, strain relief, adhesive placement and assembly instructions. We were working on the harness and the parts around it at the same time: modifying routing and wrap, protecting contact points and changing the chassis where it could damage the cable. Those changes needed to work together in the assembled product.{/* ^[evidence:EV-945341604818] */}

#### Pinching at the optical chassis

Some cable damage came from mechanical pinching and impingement during normal use, or was present straight out of assembly. I spent a great deal of time specifying secondary operations and trimming of the optics chassis, and documenting acceptable surface conditions and zones after injection molding.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}



<div data-authoring-group="chassis-contact"></div>



### Dust after assembly

With a disassembled Glyph, you could feel the air move as you extended and retracted the arms. Extension left more internal volume; retraction left less. Cycle it quickly and it pumped air through the gaps, carrying ambient dust toward the optics.

That meant a clean optical subassembly could become contaminated inside a nearly finished product. The optics-module assembly had its own dust problems. Cleanroom conditions and procedures largely brought that source under control, and every optical subassembly was inspected, with affected assemblies sent for rework or scrap. Some particles also came from materials inside the product. Around the folding mirrors, blackout-tape trimmings were a source of particles. The team's change to painted mirror edges made cleaning and rework easier.

I tightened the fits and tolerances between the stationary headband parts and the sliding earcan parts to reduce the gaps. I also added Kapton tape around the optical module to reduce the openings for particles. We never completely eliminated the pumping.

I changed the test order as well. Final optical checks moved into the final clean-area functional tests, after all the other physical functional tests. The optical inspection then checked the condition left by the operations that could contaminate the unit.







## Diagnosing returned units

Diagnosing and mitigating field failures took a substantial amount of my time. A dead headset, a dark display or missing audio was a starting symptom. The investigation could take us through the mechanical assembly, cables, electronics and optics before we understood what needed to change.{/* ^[evidence:EV-bfcb8c6e79d0] */}

### Reproducing the reported problem

One returned unit was reported to have no left-channel audio and static. The March 2016 bench report found audio working in both analog and HDMI modes, but reproduced the static. Its recommendation was to check the twist and wrapping of the cable bundles entering the input board. The reproduced problem was narrower than the original complaint, and it led back to a physical detail of the assembly.{/* ^[evidence:EV-b167a367f5e3] */}

Production board analysis showed why similar display symptoms could require different fixes. In November 2015, the team restored a striped-display board by cleaning an oxidized DMD connector pad. Other boards worked after shorted components were replaced. In one repair, a 33-ohm resistor had been replaced with 180 kilohms; correcting it brought the board back. These were specific diagnoses and repairs, rather than one explanation for every nonworking display.{/* ^[evidence:EV-45a665a2ad1f] */}

My mechanical work overlapped those electrical investigations. In April, cable wear at the chassis was suspected in two MCU shorts, and I was responsible for the mechanical response. That work fed into the clearance, protection and routing changes described above. Cycling, electrical checks and teardown each told us something different about the resulting assembly.{/* ^[evidence:EV-caf0e7c1af31] */}

### What the return records captured

A return-analysis log covering December 19, 2016 through January 12, 2017, during my final weeks at Avegant, gives a useful snapshot of the range of findings.{/* ^[evidence:EV-51df7190f9f8] */} It contains 292 observations across 290 recorded serial numbers: 220 observations marked failed and 72 marked passed. Two serials occur in both groups. The failed observations break down by their recorded primary code:

| Recorded finding | Observations |
| --- | ---: |
| Particles | 131 |
| Detached eyecan | 19 |
| Collapsed lens | 18 |
| Power | 13 |
| Boot | 7 |
| Other primary codes | 32 |

These are observations in one return-analysis log, not a failure rate for all shipped Glyphs. A unit could have more than one symptom, so its primary code was only part of the diagnostic picture. The mix shows why the work extended beyond whether a unit switched on: optical contamination, physical retention and electrical behavior all needed attention.{/* ^[evidence:EV-a7ae90028350] */}

### Feeding the findings back into production

The factory feedback included incoming parts, assembly processes and the inspection sequence. Weekly reports record a revised mirror carrier entering production and a requirement to hold products in the QC room for seven days before first-article inspection and shipment. They also record supplier corrective-action follow-up for PCB via deviations, and confirmation that a new shutter mold could meet its 90–93 degree bend-angle requirement. Those were local, practical corrections while other investigations continued.{/* ^[evidence:EV-760c604347f8] */}

For my dust work, moving the final optical check after the physical tests was part of that feedback loop. We needed to inspect the condition left by assembly and exercising the product, then use what we found to change the parts, protection or process.{/* ^[evidence:EV-de52939d1964] */}
