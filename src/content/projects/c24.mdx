          body: |
            When I took ownership of "Curtis" (C|24), I wasn't tasked with a cosmetic refresh. This was a business-critical rescue mission driven by three hard constraints:
            <ul class="list-disc pl-4 mt-2 space-y-1 text-sm">
              <li><strong>The RoHS Deadline:</strong> The legacy unit contained lead. My design had to be a ground-up re-architecture to meet strict EU compliance.</li>
              <li><strong>The Royalty Kill:</strong> We replaced the licensed preamps (~$200/unit) with an in-house design to recapture margin while holding the $9,995 price point.</li>
              <li><strong>The "Low Profile" Shift:</strong> moving from "studio furniture" to a sleek, sub-7mm aesthetic forced me to completely rethink how PCBs and thermals fit inside a significantly tighter chassis.</li>
            </ul>

        - title: "The Architect"
          subtitle: "Technical Diplomacy"

            <ul class="list-disc pl-4 mt-2 space-y-1 text-sm">
              <li><strong>Packaging Architecture:</strong> Fitting 16 preamps and a 5.1 monitor section into a chassis significantly smaller than its predecessor.</li>
              <li><strong>Vendor Management:</strong> Primary liaison for VTech/Jetcrown (Overseas) and Mass Precision (Domestic), approving First Articles.</li>
              <li><strong>Cross-Functional Leadership:</strong> Enforcing "keep-out" zones with PCB designers to prevent collisions.</li>

        - title: "The Crisis"
          subtitle: "P/N 9440-55165"
          body: |
            <strong>The Problem: Paint-Induced Warping</strong><br/>
            During Pilot, the side caps arrived with severe dimensional issues. Inspection reports showed the overall length was out of tolerance by up to **2.27mm (Shrinkage)** due to the "Rubberized Paint" cure cycle.
            <br/><br/>
            <strong>The Root Cause:</strong><br/>
            The vendor (Jetcrown) was placing these long, heavy ABS parts flat on wire racks ("Method C"). Gravity caused the softened plastic to sag and creep during the bake.

        - title: "The Solution"
          subtitle: "Forensic DOE"
          body: |
             <strong>The "Method A vs Method B" Showdown</strong><br/>
             I didn't just guess; I proved it. I rejected the vendor's support-block proposal ("Method B") and designed a comparative study.
             <br/><br/>
             <strong>The Verdict:</strong><br/>
             My "vertical hanging" theory ("Method A") utilized gravity to straighten the part during cool-down, restoring the dimension to 756.50mm. I didn't invent gravity; I invented the <strong>process control</strong> that harnessed it to save the schedule.

        - title: "The Architect"
          subtitle: "Physical Reality"
          body: |
            While Industrial Design set the vision, my role was to architect the physical reality. This required aggressive intervention in sheet metal fabrication, complex surfacing for the "Gills," and strict DFM governance over electrical layout to ensure assembly was possible.

        - title: "Sheet Metal Rescue"
          subtitle: "The 'No-Bid' Crisis"
          body: |
            The C|24 chassis was a high-precision frame requiring hundreds of welded standoffs.
            <br/><br/>
            <strong>The Crisis:</strong> Our overseas vendor (Kwanta) "no-bid" the complex Top/Bottom panels during Pilot, threatening the schedule.
            <br/><br/>
            <strong>My Solution:</strong> I executed a rapid dual-source strategy:
            <ul class="list-disc pl-4 mt-2 space-y-1 text-sm">
              <li><strong>Emergency Fabrication:</strong> Engaged Mass Precision (Local) for manual prototypes to keep the line moving.</li>
              <li><strong>The Quality War:</strong> Rejected early lots with "ripples" on bend lines and enforced a tooling change to maintain the "Pro" aesthetic.</li>
            </ul>

        - title: "The 'Gill'"
          subtitle: "Advanced Surfacing"
          body: |
            The Side Gill (P/N 9440-55175) was a signature ID element with complex "lumpy" curves that the vendor's CAD failed to interpret, leaving unsightly gaps.
            <br/><br/>
            <strong>My Execution:</strong> I took direct ownership of the surfacing, manually rebuilding the transitions in Pro/Engineer. I exported geometry in "bite-sized" STEP chunks to ensure the vendor could process the tooling paths without data loss, turning a modeling nightmare into a seamless assembly.

        - title: "PCB DFM"
          subtitle: "Collision Prevention"
          body: |
            With zero room for error, I served as gatekeeper for 17+ PCBs. My 3D analysis saved multiple board spins:
            <ul class="list-disc pl-4 mt-2 space-y-1 text-sm">
              <li><strong>MicPre Collision:</strong> Caught a vertical connector crash; forced a move to Right-Angle.</li>
              <li><strong>SubMix Ergonomics:</strong> Moved ribbon headers to the edge so factory workers could actually reach them.</li>
              <li><strong>Time Code Alignment:</strong> Found LED displays floating 8mm too high; forced a layout change to align them with the chassis window.</li>
            </ul>
