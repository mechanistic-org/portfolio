## Designing the complete player

I designed and engineered Kaleidescape’s KPLAYER-300, known internally as Mercury. The compact player had to work in a rack shelf and behind a display, which we expected to be one of its most common installations.

My work spanned the enclosure surfacing and blinded vent pattern, the EMI liners and Acheson coating on the plastics, the bezel glow board, and the main PCB layout and placement. I also designed the custom feet, fans, cooling system and fan grommets.

## Openings without exposing the interior

The enclosure used a blinded, labyrinth vent pattern. It gave the box ventilation openings while concealing the interior from view. The venting was part of the product’s appearance as well as its cooling layout.

## A continuous shield inside a plastic enclosure

The metallic liners and the coating worked together. The Acheson coating on the plastic parts tied the liners together into an integral Faraday-cage shield.

## Tuning the blue glow and isolating the fans

I tuned the glow board’s LED spacing and offset to meet Kaleidescape’s strict blue-glow visual identity. The fan mounts addressed a different aspect of the product experience: elastomeric grommets isolated the fans, reducing noise and improving noise, vibration and harshness.

## Reviewing the enclosure as a system

On July 7, 2008, I prepared the Mercury plastics review with mechanical, hardware and manufacturing colleagues. We reviewed the appearance and fit of the enclosure alongside its shielding interfaces: fan-grommet fit, vent fill, surface-resistivity test locations and crush ribs intended to push the fan bulkhead and rear panel inward. Argyle was still developing the crush-rib change, while Greg and Argyle were working through the resistivity-test redlines.

## Power supplies and the rear-shield interface

Initial power-supply problems forced us to evaluate several types, with different bodies, connections and cable routes to accommodate in the shelf.

During EMI testing in June 2008, Greg Curry and Adrian reported a LAN-conducted emissions pass after isolating the Ethernet connector from the metal rear plate. Gabe Cohn supplied revised shield CAD, and I compared the old and new geometry as the clearance requirement moved into the mechanical design. The reported pass applied to the configuration under test.

## Changing the mounting hardware

The existing mounting screw protruded too far, while shortening its thread raised manufacturability concerns. I developed replacement hardware options and updated the design files and KPLAYER-300 BOM, considering rework, a clinch stud and an inner-screw arrangement.

Michael Clader locked the new records and took on the ECO follow-up. Amiee Rooney asked to keep the existing bracket and screws available while the replacement went through prototype review. After sample feedback, I clarified the clinch-stud arrangement with the supplier. Later assembly questions still required the intended mating parts and release reference to be made explicit.

## Testing the required installation

The shelf configuration was part of the product requirement. In August 2008, Nathan Forster reported a Mercury thermal pass in a crowded rack, then reported no component-temperature difference between the shelf venting arrangements he tested. That result supported considering a simpler shelf; it did not make the player’s own case vents unnecessary.

The installation drawings also had to communicate the stack. After a dealer interpreted the height as accumulating with each shelf, Linus Wong asked for a stacked view and I supplied a revised drawing candidate. The review addressed clearance above the top shelf as well as individual dimensions.

## From KPLAYER-300 to M300

KPLAYER-300 quickly evolved into the M300 during production. The physical products were virtually identical; the processor architecture changed to support Blu-ray playback. The mechanical design carried forward as the media platform evolved.
