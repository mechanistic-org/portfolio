# SC48: making an integrated console fit

SC48 brought the host computer, audio processing and local I/O into a single live-sound console. I was brought in to work across the product's architecture, design, engineering and validation, and get it into manufacturing quickly. I led mechanical engineering and industrial design: the enclosure, thermal packaging, sheet-metal and plastic parts, and the mechanical interfaces between them. Electrical design, software, validation, sourcing and contract manufacturing were shared responsibilities. {/* ^[evidence:EV-7048bcc783b7] */}

The difficult part was keeping those systems compatible as the product changed. More room for cooling could change electromagnetic behavior. A control-surface revision could move the board beneath it. A side cover could change the airflow enough to invalidate an otherwise workable test arrangement. The engineering record follows those interactions from early packaging studies through detailed interface and fabrication work. {/* ^[evidence:LK-179274e6] ^[evidence:LK-96661432] ^[evidence:LK-847f6299] */}

## One enclosure, a high-reuse architecture

The February 2007 requirements preferred all processing and I/O inside the console, with a separate rack allowed if integration proved impractical. The cost target was below $15,000 COGS. Mechanical assumptions added side cooling and no aluminum extrusions. These were design requirements and planning assumptions, not achieved production figures. {/* ^[evidence:LK-179274e6] ^[evidence:LK-d654ff71] */}

The June 21 high-reuse plan shows what had to fit: a Mini-ITX host, PCI extender, mix-engine boards, Hub48 and audio I/O, alongside the control-surface electronics and power distribution. Reuse reduced the amount of new electronics to develop, but it left a substantial physical integration problem. Board outlines, connector access, mounting and cooling all had to work inside the same package. The diagram is an early planning architecture; it does not identify the final host or every board that shipped. {/* ^[evidence:LK-f269d5c5] */}

## Cooling had more than one acceptance condition

The surviving thermal log includes a 75°C shutdown observation. The study compared 17 configurations across chassis height, rake, fan size and voltage, filters and side covers. The mechanical team was testing complete arrangements, not simply choosing a larger fan. {/* ^[evidence:LK-38a9bda1] ^[evidence:LK-33c08f0c] */}

One useful comparison is the pair of 4U configurations with three 80 mm fans. The measurement point below is **left R69**; ambient temperature, rise above ambient and absolute temperature are separate values.

| Measurement | Fans at 8.5 V | Fans at 12 V |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Ambient | 24.3°C | 22.6°C |
| Left R69 rise | 61.3°C | 51.4°C |
| Left R69 absolute | 85.6°C | 74.0°C |

The later reports show why temperature alone could not settle the enclosure. A late-November 4U rake trial, using one 80 mm and one 120 mm fan, was reported thermally and acoustically acceptable. It still had significant electromagnetic noise, with the power supply under the analog-to-digital converters. That mixed-fan arrangement was distinct from the numerical comparison above. {/* ^[evidence:LK-4c45a3af] */}

December testing returned to 3U. Four-fan crossflow worked without the filter and side cover but failed with them fitted. Front-to-back airflow with three internal 80 mm fans worked at both 12 V and 8.5 V, and preliminary EM behavior was better than the 4U arrangement. Acoustic verification was still due; a hybrid with a 3U back and 4U front was also being tested. These reports document the alternatives being tested before the final choice. {/* ^[evidence:LK-96661432] */}

The duct record makes the physical work tangible. Die-cut, scored and formed LDPE parts guided air through the package; a later quote grouped three ducts, a filter and a gasket as a kit. The ducts, enclosure openings and cosmetic parts belonged in the same evaluation because they jointly defined the available airflow path. {/* ^[evidence:LK-d654ff71] ^[evidence:LK-e6fea936] */}

We ultimately chose 4U because it worked. SC48 went on to pass EMI certification and ship. That is my recollection of the final outcome; the selected engineering records do not identify the exact shipped fan/filter revision or the final acoustic qualification. {/* ^[evidence:EV-7048bcc783b7] */}

## The control surface was a mechanical interface

The surface layout controlled more than appearance. A January keepout overlay explicitly prohibited through-hole components in a region beneath the artwork. The annotation records a clearance constraint; it does not prove that an assembled unit had collided there. {/* ^[evidence:LK-c2323dde] */}

The interface drawings could not be finished independently of the product layout. In January, control-surface PCB DCD work was waiting for the final surface arrangement. By February, I was incorporating revised artwork into CAD, printing the layout at full scale for Product Marketing, and working through board outlines and connector placement with the electronics team. The mechanical definition had to keep the visible controls and the board beneath them aligned. {/* ^[evidence:LK-f3114028] ^[evidence:LK-e6fea936] */}

The main-left Design Control Document, DCD-9150-58331-00, shows that progression directly. Its revision history runs from initial release on February 25 through artwork and layout alignment, a mounting-hole deletion and 10-pin connector move, then three mounting-hole moves on April 7, 2008. Those are specific physical interfaces under revision, not a count of circuit designs attributed to mechanical engineering. {/* ^[evidence:LK-847f6299] */}

The same coordination extended to suppliers. The initial PCI-extender DCD went to Portwell in December. The compact PCI-frame render shown below illustrates one part definition from the visual index and cost record; the earlier prototype drawing carrying the same part number depicts a different, full-width back panel. The image is captioned as the compact frame rather than used to reconstruct a final enclosure assembly. {/* ^[evidence:LK-892b6799] ^[evidence:LK-2b5acc03] ^[evidence:LK-d654ff71] ^[evidence:LK-36b055d6] */}

## Controls, plastics and access developed together

The external parts were still being negotiated alongside the electronics. Three plastic approaches were developed for review; the selected direction used left and right side covers and a front bolster, without a rear trim piece. Subsequent work included the ACS encoder ring, the side-cover slot for a mouse tray, and the bolster's relationship to the CD drive. {/* ^[evidence:LK-b77006a3] ^[evidence:LK-70387c92] */}

The March reports distinguish alternatives from decisions. Full-size and slimline CD versions of the bolster were being prepared for SLA evaluation. Several ACS encoder versions followed. By late March, the third encoder design had been released for quotation and prototype work, and the slimline CD had been selected for further detailing. The pictured cover and bolster establish their component geometry; their render colors and shapes do not, by themselves, identify the final finish or CD revision. {/* ^[evidence:LK-70387c92] ^[evidence:LK-c5ba40c0] ^[evidence:LK-2c61a0cc] */}

## Structure and the cost of making it

The no-extrusion assumption directed the structural work toward fabricated sheet metal. The prototype 4U rake-back I-beam, P/N 9420-58317-00, shows a folded, perforated section with a 1.5 mm material note. Its October 2007 drawing is marked prototype, prepared by `pd` and checked by `en`. It supports the structural approach without assigning every drawing to one author or declaring that prototype to be the shipped chassis. {/* ^[evidence:LK-36b055d6] */}

The cost model kept CNC and hard-tool scenarios separate. One sheet-metal piece-part model totaled $521.46 plus $40 contingency, or $561.46 per unit. The $121.70 extrusion category belonged to the Profile benchmark. Removing extrusions from SC48's assumptions did not establish that amount as realized savings. {/* ^[evidence:LK-4648f58c] ^[evidence:LK-de32ae27] */}

Plastics had a separate tooling account: $36,000 assigned to the left side-cover tool, no separate right-side tooling charge, and $30,000 for the bolster. That allocation supports a shared side-cover tool interpretation. It does not establish a canceled invoice, final supplier selection or an achieved production cost. {/* ^[evidence:LK-d654ff71] */}

The delivery work continued through corrected sheet-metal packages, quote comparisons and supplier feedback. February reporting records the corrected documentation package delivered for sourcing; March reporting records additional quotes and continued incorporation of sheet-metal feedback. Quotation, prototype release and production qualification remain different milestones. {/* ^[evidence:LK-e6fea936] ^[evidence:LK-2c61a0cc] */}

## What the work delivered

<span id="shared-sc48-outcome"></span>

The documented contribution spans enclosure and cooling studies, mechanical/electrical interface control, control-surface and plastic development, and fabrication definition. The clearest result is a connected engineering record: test arrangements informed packaging choices, physical constraints reached the board layouts, and changing controls and component interfaces were carried into successive drawings and supplier packages. {/* ^[evidence:LK-96661432] ^[evidence:LK-847f6299] ^[evidence:LK-e6fea936] */}

SC48 became a commercial integrated VENUE console. Avid's later systems datasheet describes the local-I/O SC48 separately from SC48 Remote. My recollection adds the qualitative endpoint: the team chose 4U, passed EMI certification and shipped. Once Arndt and manufacturing had it handled, I moved on to other work at Digidesign. The exact handoff date and final supplier/process record remain unclaimed, along with the precise internal revisions, final acoustic results and specific EMI remedies. {/* ^[evidence:EV-7048bcc783b7] */} [Avid VENUE systems datasheet](https://cdn-www.avid.com/-/media/avid/files/generic-products-pdf/systems_ds_a4_04_25_2013.pdf).
