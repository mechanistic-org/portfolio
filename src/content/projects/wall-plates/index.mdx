Can you explain the difference between RSS and Worst Case tolerance analysis in the snap design?

Based on the forensic engineering logs for the Waldo project, the difference between Worst Case (WC) and Root Sum Squared (RSS) analysis was the deciding factor between a design that appeared broken and one that was viable for mass production.
Here is the breakdown of how these methodologies diverged in the snap design:
1. The Methodology War
• Worst Case (WC): This method assumes a "perfect storm" where every single component in the assembly measures at its maximum or minimum allowable limit simultaneously. It is an arithmetic sum of the tolerances. In the Waldo logs, this is explicitly noted as the standard for short tolerance loops: "Worst Case tolerance are used when loop is 3 or fewer parts".
• Root Sum Squared (RSS): This method assumes tolerances follow a normal statistical distribution (Bell curve). It assumes that it is statistically unlikely for a thick chassis to be paired with a short snap and a high rib all at the same time. It calculates the square root of the sum of the squared tolerances, resulting in a tighter, more optimistic tolerance window.
2. Case Study: The Z-Axis Snap Hazard
The critical nature of this distinction is visible in the Z-Axis Snap Engagement analysis (will the snap click, or will it be blocked?):
• The Stack: The loop consisted of three parts:
1. Chassis Material Thickness (-1.20mm)
2. Cover Plate Rib Height (-2.71mm)
3. Cover Plate Snap Height (4.07mm).
• The Worst Case (WC) Verdict: FAILURE
◦ Calculation: 0.16 ± 0.20
◦ Result: A minimum clearance of -0.04mm.
◦ Implication: In the worst-case scenario, the parts interfere. The snap would physically collide with the chassis before locking into place, leading to a failure to seat. The engineering note advised: "Snaps may not fully engage/seat. May need to tighten tolerances on dwgs".
• The RSS Verdict: PASS
◦ Calculation: 0.16 ± 0.12
◦ Result: A minimum clearance of +0.04mm.
◦ Implication: Statistically, the vast majority of units will have a positive gap, meaning they will assemble correctly. The calculated variation drops from 0.20mm (WC) to 0.12mm (RSS), eliminating the theoretical interference.
3. The "Y-Axis" Safety Margin
In contrast, the Y-Axis analysis (do the snaps align with the holes?) passed both methods easily, confirming the robust nature of that specific geometry:
• WC: Minimum clearance of 0.24mm.
• RSS: Minimum clearance of 0.36mm.
• Conclusion: "Snaps will always go through holes".
Summary
For the Waldo project, Worst Case analysis exposed the "hard stops"—theoretical failures that could stop shipment if tolerances drifted. RSS provided the "production reality," estimating actual yield. Because the snap loop had only 3 parts, the engineers prioritized the Worst Case findings, recommending tighter drawing tolerances to eliminate the -0.04mm interference risk.

What caused the label adhesion failures in black and white?

Based on the forensic reliability logs, the label failures on the Sativa and Elvis units were a compound failure of chemical durability (ink adhesion) and mechanical retention (substrate adhesion).
I. THE INK FAILURE (Chemical & Abrasion)
The "White" and "Black" labels suffered catastrophic yield loss during regression testing due to poor ink bonding.
• The Trigger: The labels failed the Gasoline (n-hexane) resistance test and Scratch validation.
• The Data:
◦ Gasoline Test: "Label can't readable" — 3/3 White units and 3/3 Black units failed. The solvent dissolved the print, rendering the regulatory text illegible.
◦ Scratch Test: On the black labels, the "white ink scratches away from black label," failing the durability mandate.
• The Root Cause: The initial vendor's printing process could not withstand the chemical attack or physical abrasion required for the "Class C" surface specifications.
II. THE SUBSTRATE FAILURE (Edge Lifting)
Simultaneously, the labels physically detached from the device housing (Defect 710-00011).
• The Trigger: "Bottom inner edges/corners lift." The labels peeled up at the stress points created by the "SN notch" design.
• The Component Failure: The original adhesive, 3M 200 (467), failed to bond effectively to the "Fine Matte" / "Satin VDI 15" finish of the polycarbonate housing.
• The Intervention:
◦ Adhesive Swap: The engineering team mandated a transition to 3M 300LSE (9453LE), a high-strength acrylic adhesive designed for low-surface-energy (LSE) plastics.
◦ Geometry Edit: Proposals were executed to "Modify outline to eliminate SN notch" and "Remove SN recess" from the tooling to reduce peel stress.
III. THE GEOMETRY CONFLICT (Process Failure)
A third failure mode emerged regarding the application of the Serial Number (SN).
• The Conflict: The factory (FIH) could not print the SN on the main label on-demand because the label thickness (0.15mm) was "too thick to print" without causing barcode deviation.
• The Fallout: This forced a design divergence, requiring either a separate SN label (0.07mm) or a "window" cut in the adhesive liner to allow back-printing.

• Observation: The film was "floating up" from the surface, creating visible "bubbles under the film".
• Implication: This separation allowed air pockets to form, likely weakening the bond between the adhesive and the film backing while the adhesive remained wet out on the plastic surface.
2. Adhesive Transfer (The Result) When the film was removed, the adhesive failed cohesively or adhesively at the film-backing interface rather than the device interface.
• The Defect: "Glue remain on the surface".
• Severity: This required manual cleaning intervention or resulted in scrapped parts, effectively negating the purpose of the protective film. The defect was flagged as a "Known issue" in the packaging reviews.

• Abrasion Risks: The packaging tests also exposed "switch tray cornor abrasion," necessitating the protective film in the first place. We were trapped in a loop: the packaging caused abrasion, so we added film; the film failed, leaving glue residue.

Tell me more about the 'Pillowed Dimple' defect on the steel chassis.

Based on the forensic engineering logs, the "Pillowed Dimple" defect (Log ID: WAL-15) was a specific metal deformation issue identified during the manufacturing of the Waldo chassis (the stamped steel mounting plate).
I. The Mechanism of Failure
• The Feature: The chassis design included "Large square dimples" stamped into the steel. Their functional purpose was to maintain the correct spacing for the Bazooka unit's slide-lock hooks, ensuring the main controller module sat flush and locked securely.
• The Defect: The stamping process for these square shapes was too aggressive for the material (SPCC Steel). This caused the metal in the "immediate surrounding areas" to deform or "pillow," creating an unintentional curvature or stress distortion.
• The Impact: This deformation occurred directly at the slide-lock interface. Because the metal was warped ("pillowed"), it interfered with the slide-lock mechanism, making it difficult to engage or creating an inconsistent fit for the Bazooka module.
II. The Engineering Intervention
To resolve this for the EVT2 build, the engineering team determined that the shape of the dimple was causing the stress concentration, while only the depth was functionally critical.
• The Fix: The design directive was to "minimize to round pip."
• The Rationale: Changing the geometry from a large square to a "smaller circular dimple" (or round pip) at the same depth would distribute the stamping stress more evenly, eliminating the "pillowing" effect while still providing the necessary clearance for the slide-lock hooks.
• DFM Validation: This change was corroborated in the Chassis DFM (Design for Manufacturing) feedback, which explicitly noted "Rounded dimples to reduce stresses".
