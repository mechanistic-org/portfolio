# Wall Plates: turning a clean exterior into a tolerance decision

## Project summary

- **Role boundary:** Mechanical product architecture and reliability oversight for the Waldo cover/chassis system and its interface to the retained module. The surviving detailed tolerance workbooks credit Colin Davis as engineer; this page does not attribute those calculations to Erik.
- **Mandate:** Make a screwless molded cover fit a stamped multi-gang chassis, seat its snaps, hold the internal Bazooka module, and preserve a controlled exterior gap across manufacturing variation.
- **Core result:** The analysis separated conditions that earlier prose collapsed. X and Y entry clearances remained positive, but minimum engagement was only 0.3 mm, Z seating could cross zero, and the module-retention gap could also collapse to zero.

## The visible surface hid three interfaces

A screwless wall plate looks like one cosmetic part. Mechanically it is at least three coupled systems: the perimeter gap between cover and chassis, the cover snaps entering and engaging stamped openings, and the internal module sitting behind a molded retaining rib.

Treating those systems as one `fit` question creates false confidence. A snap can pass through its opening and still fail to seat in Z. A cover can fit its chassis while the module behind it loses retention margin. The surviving workbooks are valuable because they keep those questions separate.

## Perimeter clearance was positive, with limits

The detailed perimeter sheet uses a worst-case stack for the molded cover and stamped chassis. For the two-gang configuration it records a 0.48 mm nominal condition, 0.23 mm minimum, and 0.73 mm maximum. The workbook notes that a similar four-gang construction `should be OK`; that is an engineering expectation, not evidence that every gang count was verified.

This distinction replaces the legacy claim of guaranteed flawless alignment. The stack demonstrates a bounded two-gang clearance. It does not prove visual acceptance, production yield, or every larger configuration.

## Clearance is not engagement

The snap workbook separates X, Y, and Z behavior. In X, the worst-case result retains 0.9 mm on each side. In Y, entry clearance remains 0.24 mm, leading to the narrow conclusion that the snaps can pass through the openings. But Y engagement falls to 0.3 mm per snap at the minimum condition.

Z is the sharper risk. Its worst-case range runs from -0.04 to 0.36 mm, and the workbook explicitly warns that the snaps may not fully engage or seat. The analysis therefore does not certify the mechanism. It tells the team where drawing tolerances or nominal geometry must absorb the remaining risk.

## The inner interface was a second system

The Bazooka-to-Waldo retention workbook analyzes the die-cast module, stamped chassis, and molded retaining rib as a separate stack. The nominal maximum-gap chain is 0.84 mm, with a 0.54 mm worst-case minimum. The minimum-gap chain is nominally 0.30 mm and can reach effectively zero under worst case.

That does not prove a retention failure. It proves that the lower bound has no worst-case gap margin. By exposing it separately, the program could decide whether to change the rib, the chassis slot, the module tooth, or the controlled tolerances instead of asking the cosmetic cover to absorb an invisible system problem.

## Quantified impact

- **Bounded** the two-gang perimeter at 0.23 to 0.73 mm under worst case.
- **Preserved** 0.9 mm X clearance per side and 0.24 mm Y entry clearance.
- **Exposed** a 0.3 mm minimum Y engagement condition.
- **Identified** a Z seating range of -0.04 to 0.36 mm instead of declaring the snaps guaranteed.
- **Separated** the Bazooka-retention chain, whose worst-case minimum gap reaches zero.
- **Corrected** public authorship to credit the engineer named in the source workbook.
