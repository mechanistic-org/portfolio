# 320-Slot Carousel

A disc vault has to bring one disc out of a dense array, hand it to the transport, and return it without catching an edge. Kaleidescape's 320-slot carousel made that repeated interface part of one large molding. Slot shape, molded dimensions, carousel alignment and roller behavior all affected the same operation.

My work at Kaleidescape included tooling-change decisions, part definition, inspection and production support. The work crossed several teams: Function Engineering developed the early mechanical breadboards; Loyd May contributed the rib and radius changes used in the molding plan; manufacturing partners ran the trials; and Nathan Forster's later system tests helped distinguish slot problems from transport behavior.

## Establishing the geometry

The early program tested the mechanism before committing its geometry to a production tool. Function Engineering's August 2010 phase report records a breadboard platter with interchangeable pie wedges for four slot geometries, followed by a full SLA carousel. Its tests demonstrated repeatable disc handling at the breadboard level and explored alignment among the carousel, kicker and pinch rollers. Encoder/control confirmation and component life testing remained follow-on work.

The slot's V-groove constrained the disc and aligned it with the kicker. The perimeter also acted as a large timing pulley, so belt engagement brought another tolerance problem: the long contact length accumulated pitch variation. Function's report called for slightly wider pulley grooves and considered omitting some teeth. Its selected belt and pulley combination provided an integral number of motor steps per disc slot. These were mechanical design decisions with consequences for indexing, packaging and manufacture, well before the later molding problems.

## Turning the geometry into a molding

The January 2011 trials showed how far a working prototype was from an acceptable molded part. T1 produced extensive flash after intentional over-pressure was used to try to fill the tool, with broken ribs left in the cavity. The development report also recorded brittle material behavior, but explicitly left open whether incorrect processing contributed.

T3 produced 46 ABS and 46 HIPS samples after moving the tool to a trial specialist. My report described an encouraging functional check in some slots while identifying ejector deformation, knit lines and dimensions still requiring inspection. It was progress, with further work plainly visible.

## Revising the tool

The pre-T4 plan brought together Loyd May's additional support rib, increased rib widths and larger blending radii. We treated those as the first incremental changes; more extensive measures, including vacuum venting, remained alternatives to evaluate. The $2,500 and one-week figures were a preliminary quote for that modification package.

By T4, the part was filling consistently, but the report still recorded undersize parts, air traps, short shots and flash. T5 showed no significant improvement; T6 still had undersize parts and air-entrapment marks. The April 19 timeline listed T7 as scheduled. Fill consistency was one useful result within an unfinished dimensional and process problem.

## Finding the variation within 320 slots

A carousel could look complete while a particular arc behaved differently. The 2011 investigation combined physical measurements with optical inventory scans to locate troublesome slots. At slot 205, the report measured 1.37 mm just ahead of the V-groove, against its stated 1.50 mm intent. Local rib thickness and the disc's own edge geometry mattered to the available clearance.

The scans localized poor responses, then the tooling investigation examined differences among sections. Re-burning or replacing individual sections was proposed. A later slot-120 request compared insert replacement with repair and re-burn work. These records preserve the distinction between a measured defect, a suspected tooling cause and a proposed repair.

## Defining rework and acceptance

Temporary rework needed its own acceptance method. The October procedure described removing interfering material and cleaning the slots. The November inspection required three test discs to pass through every one of the 320 positions under their own weight. Damaged parts were rejected, and reworked slots had to be checked again. Drawing maintenance continued too: the January 2012 Rev 11 ECN corrected a minus-tolerance typo and gate numbering. The later tooling proposals also made gate identification useful to operators correlating controller output with physical gate drops.

## Supporting the part in production

Many of these changes reached production during a compressed sequence of programs, with design-for-manufacture work continuing on the fly.

The production problem extended beyond the tool. The April 2014 packaging instruction enlarged the pocket around the carousel to relieve interference with the packing material. A separate warpage procedure established a flat reference, marked high points and a reject/quarantine limit. These gave receiving and production staff a repeatable way to judge parts.

## Testing the disc path and deciding disposition

The adjacent disc path was also under investigation. Nathan's April 17 tests combined a modified guide and upper bearing with stronger springs. Double force passed four run-in cycles in the reported tests, including dirty-roller and known rib-hit conditions. Triple force caused the spring itself to take a permanent set. My April 21 plan called for the guide/bearing changes and double-force springs, with solid bushings and a modified gear to be tested and implemented if necessary. The April 29 release discussion still called for testing the spring prototypes before their release.

Part disposition remained a separate responsibility. In August, I explicitly directed failed parts to be scrapped. By January 2015, a replacement carousel tool was again under quotation review, with improved spacing consistency an objective. The reviewed records stop before that tool's final disposition.

The engineering work was the connection among these decisions: proving a slot geometry, making it repeatably, measuring where it varied, testing the disc interface and giving production a defensible acceptance rule. The carousel was one component, but its performance depended on that entire chain.
