I. PROJECT SUMMARY
• Role: Lead Mechanical Engineer ("The Architect")
• Mandate: Engineer a high-density, 320-slot optical storage mechanism capable of precision robotic loading within a consumer appliance footprint, overcoming the inherent thermal instability of large-diameter injection molding.
• Core Achievement: Stabilized yield for a 386mm diameter complex part, effectively managing thermal shrinkage and warpage to secure a $2,500 tooling recovery and long-term field reliability.
II. THE ANATOMY OF FAILURE (Heuristic Analysis)
Discovery Heuristics applied to T-Shot Logs and Field Failure Data.

1. Thermal Crisis (The Molding Struggle)
   • The Trigger (Crisis): The T1 shots (Jan 2011) were a catastrophic failure. The molder used "intentional over-pressure" to fill the tool, resulting in "massive flash" and broken ribs stuck in the cavity. What are the engineering proposals to fix the Carousel warpage?

Based on the forensic engineering logs and project correspondence, the engineering proposals to address and mitigate Carousel Warpage (specifically affecting yield in the M700/DV700 Vaults) focused on three vectors: root cause prevention in logistics, mechanical compensation in the drive train, and containment via inspection.

1. Root Cause Prevention: The Packaging Redesign
   To address warpage occurring post-manufacture, the team identified shipping stress as a primary contributor.
   • Proposal: Redesign the shipping packaging used by the Taiwan manufacturer (Yomura/YungChing).
   • Logic: Forensic analysis of stock in Sunnyvale revealed warped carousels that had passed initial QC, implicating the transit environment. Tighter packaging was suspected of compressing the large-diameter parts over time.
   • Action: Erik Norris coordinated with the vendor to "loosen up" the packaging specifications to reduce compressive stress during transit.
2. Mechanical Compensation: The "Hard Mount" Overrides
   Since warpage resulted in "tight slots" (specifically in the 200–280 arc) that generated friction exceeding the pinch rollers' grip, I proposed modifying the drive train to mechanically overpower the defect rather than scrapping inventory.
   • Hard Mount Spacers: Replace the compliant rubber grommets on the drive roller shaft with hard mount spacers.
   ◦ Why: Warped slots caused high friction. The existing rubber grommets allowed the drive shaft to deflect (bend) away from the disc under load, causing slippage. Solid spacers eliminated this compliance, forcing the disc through the warped slot.
   • Modified Inner Disc Guide: Redesign the inner guide to provide physical backing support to the driven roller.
   ◦ Why: This prevented the driven roller from bending backward under the increased loads required to insert discs into warped slots.
   • Spring Force Escalation: Implement "Double Force" springs (approx. 200% increase).
   ◦ Why: To generate sufficient normal force to overcome the friction of the "tight slots" and maintain traction on slippery or dirty discs.
3. Containment & Governance
   • Enhanced Inspection Criteria (Rev 2): Development of a specific "Warpage Inspection" protocol (Rev 1 & Rev 2) requiring 100% inspection of carousels prior to installation. This included using gauge blocks and measuring vertical deflection to flag "out of spec" parts immediately.
   • Sensor Board Calibration: A proposal was made to physically adjust the sensor board to calibrate carousel position against the warpage, but this was deemed "unlikely to be effective" due to the design constraints, leading back to the mechanical hardening approach.

Why was the 'Tight Slot War' such a critical failure?

Forensic Data Extraction: The "Tight Slot" War
CONTEXT: Phase: Field Failure & Yield Crisis (2014) Target: Slot Arc 200–280 Severity: Critical (Functional Hard Stop)
I. THE PHYSICS OF FAILURE (Friction vs. Traction) The "Tight Slot War" was critical because it represented a dimensional drift that physically overpowered the mechanism's ability to function. It wasn't a software glitch; it was a battle of forces.
• The Dimensional Drift: In the specific arc of slots 200–280, rib thickness drifted to 1.00mm–1.08mm, significantly exceeding the 0.86mm nominal specification.
• The Friction Crisis: This thickening narrowed the slot width to 1.37mm (vs 1.50mm spec). When the ODD (Optical Disc Drive) attempted to inject a disc, the friction generated by these "tight slots" exceeded the traction provided by the pinch rollers.
• The Mechanical Yield: The original drive train utilized rubber grommets for compliance. Under the high-friction load of a "tight slot," the drive roller shaft deflected (bent) away from the disc rather than forcing it through.
II. THE ANATOMY OF THE CRASH The failure mode was catastrophic for a "luxury" robotic appliance because it required manual intervention to clear.
• "Lost Discs": Because the rollers lost traction, discs were left "hanging" out of the front, neither fully rejected nor inserted. This led to STATUS_VAULTMGR_CAROUSEL_BLOCKAGE_DETECTED errors, freezing the entire vault.
• The "Un-insertable" Loop: Logs revealed repeated timeouts where the system attempted to load a disc, failed due to friction, and queued it for ejection, effectively rendering that section of the carousel dead.
• Warpage Amplification: The issue was exacerbated by packaging stress during transit from Taiwan, which compressed the large-diameter parts, locking the warpage into the 200–280 arc.
III. THE ENGINEERING INTERVENTION (Mechanical Hardening) We could not simply "tune" our way out of this; we had to mechanically overpower the defect.

1. Hard Mount Protocol: I designed hard mount spacers to replace the compliant rubber grommets on the drive shaft. This eliminated shaft deflection, forcing the roller to transfer 100% of its torque into moving the disc, regardless of slot friction.
2. Force Multiplication: We increased the pinch roller spring force by 50%–200%. This massive increase in normal force ensured that even with "greasy/dirty deposits" or warped slots, the drive maintained traction.
3. Support Reinforcement: Redesigned the inner disc guide to provide a physical backstop for the driven roller, preventing it from bowing backward under load.
   How did you solve the 2014 tight slot field failures?

Based on the forensic engineering logs, solving the 2014 "Tight Slot" field failures required a shift from "tuning" the system to mechanically overpowering the defect. The variances in the molded carousel (specifically in the 200–280 slot arc) created friction levels that the original drive train—designed for compliance—could not overcome.
Here is the forensic breakdown of the solution:
I. THE PHYSICS OF THE FIX (Mechanical Hardening)
The core failure mode was shaft deflection. When the pinch rollers encountered a "tight slot" (width restricted to ~1.37mm by thickened ribs), the resistance caused the drive roller shaft to bend away from the disc rather than pushing it through. We effectively hardened the drive train to eliminate this compliance.
• Hard Mount Spacers (The "Solid Bushing" Mod):
◦ Action: Replaced the compliant rubber grommets on the drive roller shaft with solid mount spacers.
◦ Physics: This eliminated the ability of the drive shaft to deflect (bend) under high-load conditions. By removing the "give" in the system, 100% of the motor's torque was transferred into linear force on the disc, forcing it through the warped slot.
• Driven Roller Support (The "Inner Guide" Redesign):
◦ Action: Redesigned the Inner Disc Guide to provide a physical backstop for the driven (passive) roller.
◦ Physics: Previously, high spring force caused the driven roller axle to bend. The new guide acted as a structural reinforcement, preventing this deflection and maintaining the correct trajectory for the disc during insertion.
• Spring Force Escalation:
◦ Action: Implemented Double Force (200%) springs.
◦ Physics: While "Triple Force" springs were tested, they caused inelastic deformation (relaxing) of the plastic. The Double Force springs provided sufficient normal force to maintain traction on slippery or dirty discs against the friction of the tight slots without destroying the assembly.
II. ROOT CAUSE PREVENTION (The Logistics Fix)
Forensic analysis of inventory revealed that the "potato chipping" (warpage) of the 386mm carousels was exacerbated by shipping stress.
• Packaging Redesign:
◦ Diagnosis: The vacuum-formed packaging trays were too tight, applying continuous torque to the carousel geometry during transit from Taiwan.
◦ Action: Directed the vendor (Yomura) to modify the packaging tooling to "relieve pressure" on the carousels. This prevented the "locking in" of warpage stresses before the parts even reached the assembly line.
III. CONTAINMENT (The Inspection)
• Metrology & Screening: Implemented a specific inspection protocol for the 200–280 slot arc, utilizing gauge blocks to identify "tight slots" and disqualify parts where rib thickness exceeded 1.00mm (nominal was 0.86mm).

What are 'white marks' on ejection and how were they solved?

Forensic Data Extraction: Ejection White Marks
CONTEXT: Phase: T1–T6 Tooling Iteration Defect: Cosmetic/Structural Stress Whitening ("White Marks") Identity Anchor: "The Architect"
I. THE PHYSICS OF FAILURE (Stress Whitening) "White marks on ejection" are not surface contamination; they are mechanical trauma. They represent areas where the plastic underwent inelastic deformation (yielding) during the ejection stroke.
• The Mechanism: The large surface area of the 386mm carousel creates immense friction against the mold core. When the ejector pins fire, they punch into the plastic. If the material cannot release from the steel (drag), the stress concentrates at sharp corners, causing the polymer chains to craze and turn white.
• Differentiation: These are distinct from the "White burn marks" noted in T6, which were caused by air entrapment (Diesel effect) igniting the compressed gas at the end of the fill.
II. THE ENGINEERING INTERVENTION (Geometry & Polish) To stop the part from self-destructing during ejection, we altered the tool geometry to reduce drag and distribute stress.
• The "Radius" Fix: As detailed in the "Ribs & Rads" proposal and T-Shot inspections, the primary solution was to increase radii at the base of the ribs and sharp corners.
◦ Specific Action: We increased general feature radii to 2.5mm and rib radii to 1.5mm.
◦ Physics: A larger radius eliminates the stress concentrator (notch effect), allowing the force of ejection to spread through the part without exceeding the material's yield strength.
• Surface Finish: In specific areas like the vent slides, sticking was eliminated by improving the cavity polish.
• Active Ejection (Proposed): Early in the crisis (T1), we identified that the flow length and fin geometry caused "flow hesitation" and sticking. We proposed an "active" mechanism to assist ejection, though the geometric changes (Ribs & Rads) became the primary fix.
III. THE RESULT By T6, while the part still fought with air traps (burns) and dimensional shrinkage, the catastrophic "broken ribs" and structural white marks were largely mitigated by the radius modifications and process tuning.
