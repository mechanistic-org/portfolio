# KSYSTEM-120 / Orpheus

The Kaleidescape Mini System brought a movie server, playback electronics, music zones and a DVD/CD reader into one enclosure. Developed as Orpheus and sold as the KSYSTEM-120, it combined four removable disk cartridges with a front panel intended to sit beside the rest of a home's entertainment equipment.

My contribution covered mechanical integration and new-product introduction: coordinating physical builds, working through enclosure and board interfaces, supporting the front-panel Glow development, and defining how first articles would be inspected and their discrepancies resolved. I worked with Kaleidescape's electrical, hardware-program, manufacturing and quality teams, Argyle Design, and the suppliers building the parts.

## A complete system in a compact enclosure

The product combined functions that had previously occupied separate Kaleidescape components. The mechanical package had to accommodate the server and player electronics, an optical drive, four disk cartridges, a power supply and cooling hardware, while remaining usable as a freestanding component or a two-rack-unit installation.

That put the important work at the interfaces. The drive cage had to locate cartridges against the drive-plane board. The motherboard and player board needed support without losing connector alignment. Cable routes competed with the removable-drive assembly, and grounding, shielding and airflow had to survive the same assembly sequence.

The mechanical BOM makes this structure explicit: chassis base, PCB bracket, fan tray, power-supply enclosure, DVD bracket, inner and outer drive cages, front controls, gaskets and harnesses. Its assembly annotations also show the level at which integration happened: relocating fasteners, clarifying thermal-probe attachment, and changing harness routes so wires did not pass over the drive cage. These were working changes to a buildable assembly, with different revisions still visible in the record.

## Making physical builds useful

In June and July 2008, the team distinguished an early functional mechanical build from a build approaching production geometry. The early version combined sheet metal with functional prototype plastics; the later version was intended to bring metalwork and plastics toward tooled parts. Treating those as different configurations made the schedule more useful than a single promise that a prototype would be ready.

I distributed the mechanical schedule and coordinated the parts needed for build kits at Argyle and in-house. Functional boards were scarce, so rejected bare boards were to be populated with the connectors and tall components needed for mechanical evaluation. That let the mechanical team check the physical interfaces without waiting for every board to be electrically functional.

The drive enclosure illustrates why those builds mattered. A hard-drive grounding requirement prompted the team to reconsider mounting and enclosure concepts, including formed metal, plastic and magnesium-alloy alternatives. The July meeting record also identified an assembly problem: riveting the inner and outer cages together could prevent installation of the drive-plane PCB. I carried the request for an assembly and service procedure into the mechanical review. A drive cage that located the parts still needed a workable order of assembly and a way to service them.

## Closing the base-to-cover fit

Sanmina's Orpheus DFM response shows a specific problem with relying on nominal dimensions. A 15.920-inch base appeared to fit inside a 15.928-inch cover. Applying a plus-or-minus 0.015-inch tolerance to each, however, allowed a maximum base of 15.935 inches and a minimum cover opening of 15.913 inches: 0.022 inches of interference.

The reviewed alternative limited the base's positive tolerance and prevented the cover opening from falling below nominal. Both worst-case limits then met at 15.928 inches. The response marks that scheme approved. The calculation removed the stated worst-case overlap; it did not create a generous running clearance or establish the fit of every subsequently built unit.

The same review considered rolled edges versus protective grommets, formed features and threaded extrusions, cover-lip flexibility, and welding methods. Some items were approved while tooling questions remained pending. The useful distinction was between an idea, a reviewed drawing change and a physically verified part. Those stages could not be collapsed into one production-ready label.

## The Glow was a mechanical and optical interface

The blue Glow across the front was part of the product's appearance, but its uniformity depended on a tightly constrained package. The December 29, 2008 section drawing shows the LED board captured by the frame and inner panel, behind a curved screen. Changing the light pattern therefore involved the relationship between board position, screen geometry and electrical tuning.

The retained Glow presentation shows visible bright spots in one condition and a more diffuse band in another. A separate slide records resistor tuning on the right side. These are useful records of the visual problem and the tuning work. They do not provide a measured uniformity ratio or establish that either photograph represents the final shipping configuration.

## Defining the factory's acceptance criteria

The manufacturing handoff included both dimensional requirements and instructions for judging parts. I authored the June 28 draft and July 9, 2008 peer-review release of the mechanical first-article inspection procedure. It assigned responsibilities across Quality, Engineering, Manufacturing Engineering and Purchasing, and distinguished inspection from trial assembly.

When a part fell outside its drawing limits, the procedure provided two routes: change the part or process to meet the design, or revise the design documentation through the engineering change process. It also called for the affected parts and assemblies to be checked again. That is a more useful description of the work than treating the inspection form's rejection count as a product-yield metric.

Paint masking raised a related issue during the March 2009 supplier discussions. I distinguished acceptable light overspray inside the chassis from the need to keep threaded electrical-contact surfaces protected. After discussing the masking requirements with the supplier, I requested a painted sample for review. The record establishes that clarification and sample-review step; it does not establish a measured recovery in masking throughput.

The retained shop photographs below show a masked panel and a panel held in machining and clamping fixtures. They provide physical context for the enclosure work. The images alone do not identify a particular dimensional deviation or prove that a corrective operation was released to production.

## Product outcome

Kaleidescape announced the Mini System on March 3, 2009 as immediately available. Its KSYSTEM-120 datasheet identifies the four-cartridge product and its freestanding or two-rack-unit installation. That establishes the commercial product outcome independently of the earlier pilot schedules and part-level inspection records.

My documented contribution was the mechanical work connecting the product package to physical builds and supplier execution: coordinating the interfaces, making discrepancies reviewable, and defining how parts and trial assemblies would be evaluated. Electrical design, industrial design, manufacturing execution and product certification remained cross-functional responsibilities. The surviving record does not establish a production-volume or whole-product-yield result attributable to me.
