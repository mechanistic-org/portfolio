# KSYSTEM-120 (Orpheus) Forensic Report

## I. PROJECT SUMMARY

- **Role:** Senior Mechanical Design Engineer
- **Mandate:** Execute the mechanical design and manufacturing transfer of the "Mini System" (Orpheus)—a high-density A/V server bridging legacy servers and low-cost players—from prototype shops to mass production at Sanmina.
- **Core Achievement:** Salvaged a stalled 50-unit Pilot Build and enabled a 300-unit Production ramp by resolving three concurrent "stop-ship" interference crises (The Hammered Lid, The Thumb of God, and The Glow Debacle).

## II. THE ANATOMY OF FAILURE (Heuristic Analysis)

_Analysis of critical failure modes identified during the Pilot Phase._

- **The Trigger (Crisis):**
  1.  **Mechanical Violence ("The Hammered Lid"):** Assemblers were physically prying chassis lids until spot welds fractured because an out-of-spec front weldment (0.45" vs 0.300") was crushing the internal drive cage. A colleague was observed using a hammer to force a unit closed during lab testing.
  2.  **Yield Catastrophe ("The Thumb of God"):** The "Player PCB" suffered a 20% fallout rate. The sheer density of cabling and tight clearances caused the board to flex during insertion, shearing off resistor R332 and fracturing BGA solder joints, resulting in a "Gennum" communication boot loop.
  3.  **Thermal/Optical Failure:** Early units suffered "hard hangs" every 50 minutes due to missing thermal paste/heatsink interference, and the LED "Glow" was rejected for "blobby" diffusion due to proximity to the bezel.

- **The Intervention (Fix):**
  1.  **Geometric Redesign:** Engineered a 4-hole "rigidifying" mounting pattern and a "peninsula cut" on the PCB/tray to eliminate the flex inducing the BGA fractures.
  2.  **Chassis Overhaul:** Redesigned the top cover (520-1066-00) to eliminate the separate welded flange causing the stack-up interference, adding closure hooks for positive retention and reverting material to G30 steel to streamline fabrication.
  3.  **Process Control:** Mandated a strict "Go/No-Go" gauge inspection protocol at the vendor (Sanmina) to prevent out-of-spec metal from reaching the line.

- **The Result (Impact):**
  - Reduced PCB fallout from **20% to stable production levels**.
  - Eliminated the need for manual force (hammering) during assembly.
  - Negotiated CPU thermal solution unit cost down from **$9.63 to $9.25** while improving reliability.

## III. GOVERNANCE & RHYTHM

- **The Pulse:** Managed via high-frequency "War Room" coordination between Waterloo (EE/SW) and Sunnyvale (ME/Ops), serving as the single-point filter for all CAD deliverables from the ID firm (Argyle) before release to the CM.

# VI. ENGINEERING DEEP DIVES

## First Article Inspection (FAI) Protocol

Based on the engineering documentation, the **First Article Inspection (FAI)** criteria for mechanical parts were governed by a strict protocol (Document No. **FAI-ECN-001**) designed to verify that the first production unit from a vendor met all design requirements before mass production began.

The inspection process was binary: an item **passed** only if _all_ inspected attributes fell within the design tolerance limits, and **failed** if _any_ attribute was out of spec.

### 1. When FAI Was Required

An inspection was mandatory whenever any of the following triggers occurred:

- A part was supplied by a vendor for the **first time**.
- A **new revision** of an item was released.
- A change occurred in the **manufacturing process** (e.g., changing from soft tooling to hard tooling).
- A change occurred in the **tooling** itself.

### 2. The Inspection Criteria (The "What")

The inspection validated the part against three primary standards:

- **Dimensional Accuracy (The Print):**
  The core requirement was measuring specific physical dimensions against the engineering drawing. For example, on the **Orpheus Fan Mounts (330-1029-00)**, the inspection measured the width (23.5 ± 0.25mm) and height (4.2 ± 0.25mm) across multiple samples to ensure they fell within the tolerance band.
- **Cosmetic Standards (CIS-ECN-001):**
  Parts were evaluated based on their visibility to the end-user, classified into three zones:
  - **Class A (Highly Visible):** Viewed under 80-150 foot-candles of light at 18 inches for 10 seconds. No scratches, burrs, or rust were allowed. Foreign material was strictly limited (e.g., no more than 3 specks total, none larger than .010" diameter).
  - **Class B (Seldom Viewed):** Viewed at 18 inches for 5 seconds. Slight imperfections were tolerated if they fell within strict size limits.
  - **Class C (Internal/Hidden):** Viewed at 24 inches. Scratches and some discoloration were generally acceptable if they did not impact function.
- **Material Verification:**
  The composition of the material had to be verified, often requiring a Material Certificate from the vendor unless waived by engineering.

### 3. The Process and Documentation

- **The FAIR (First Article Inspection Report):** Results were recorded on a standardized data sheet (FAIR-ECN-001). This document listed the **Item Requirement** (the target dimension from the CAD/Print) next to the **Actual Measurement** for the sample lot (typically 5 to 20 units).
- **Disposition of Failure:** If a part failed (e.g., the AC Tunnel measured 43.91mm against a requirement of 44.19 ± 0.1mm), a **Material Review Board (MRB)** meeting was convened. The team had two choices:
  1.  **Reject:** The vendor must modify the tooling/process to match the design.
  2.  **Deviate/Redesign:** Engineering changes the design documentation (via ECO) to match the part if the deviation is functionally acceptable.

### 4. Application in the Orpheus Project

During the Orpheus Pilot Build, this process was critical for resolving interference issues:

- **Sanmina Metal Inspection:** Erik Norris specifically requested FAI on chassis parts like the top cover (520-1066-00) to check for "side flange edges not straight" and mandated a "Go/No-Go" gauge protocol to catch out-of-spec parts before they reached the assembly line.
- **Trial Assembly:** In addition to measuring dimensions, the team often performed a "Trial Assembly" where the first article parts were physically assembled to ensure fit and function, checking for issues like the "hammered lid" interference.

## The Glow Crisis: Optical & Mechanical Triage

Based on the engineering logs and forensic reports, I resolved the "blobby" and inconsistent glow light issue on the Orpheus project through a multi-disciplinary "squeeze" that involved mechanical re-geometry, component substitution, and electrical tuning.

Here is how I fixed it:

### 1. Mechanical Geometry: The "Flex" and Spacing

The primary cause of the "blobbiness" was that the LEDs were positioned too close to the translucent front screen (approximately 0.5mm at the ends), making the individual point sources visible rather than a diffused glow.

- **Retreating the PCB:** I coordinated a mechanical redesign to physically move the Glow PCB back away from the screen. We aimed for a minimum of **2mm to 4mm** of spacing.
- **Flexing the Board:** Because the chassis door was curved and space was tight, I worked with the ID firm (Argyle) to modify the **inner panel plastics**, scooping out "pockets" that allowed the PCB to **flex backwards** at the ends.
- **Standoffs:** I implemented **6mm standoffs** to ensure the PCB maintained this new, deeper distance from the front bezel.

### 2. Component Substitution: Low-Profile LEDs

We replaced the original "tall" Avago PLCC2 LEDs with **low-profile LEDs** (specifically **Stanley SB1118C** or similar ChipLEDs).

- **Gaining Distance:** These thinner LEDs effectively increased the distance between the emitter and the screen by another **~1mm**, further smoothing the diffusion.
- **Better Binning:** The new LEDs had a much tighter intensity tolerance (delta of &lt;9mcd vs. 19mcd), eliminating the "hot spots" caused by random brightness variations in the original parts.
- **Wider Angle:** The new LEDs had a viewing angle of 170 degrees (vs. 140), which helped blend the light between sources.

### 3. Electrical Tuning: Resistors

To address the "blotchiness" caused by voltage drop variances in the LEDs, we changed the electrical design:

- **Increased Resistance:** We increased the series resistance by a factor of roughly 40. This minimized the current variance caused by the 70mV voltage drop tolerance in the LEDs, ensuring uniform brightness.
- **Bleeder Resistors:** We added bleeder resistors to stabilize the circuit.
- **Positional Dimming:** We tuned the resistor values specifically at the ends of the array (dimming them down) to compensate for the board curvature bringing those LEDs slightly closer to the plastic than the center ones.

### 4. Optical Cleanup: The "Fence" and Paint

I identified that a structural rib on the inside of the front screen was casting a shadow line (the "fence effect") and that light was bleeding through the white plastic.

- **Rib Removal:** I directed the vendor (Steman/Weddy) to remove the interfering rib from the tool.
- **Masking:** We implemented a **grey paint mask** on the inside surfaces of the bezel frame to block light leakage and define the glow edges sharply.
  - _Note:_ While the internal paint mask was the initial proposal, it encountered manufacturing rejection due to fixture risks. The final solution involved a mechanical redesign of the grey plastic frame to creating a blocking channel.

## Cost Engineering: The Thermal Squeeze

Based on the engineering and supply chain logs, I negotiated the thermal cost down for the Orpheus project by leveraging a lower-cost third-party alternative to force the primary motherboard vendor (Kontron) to lower their price.

Here is the step-by-step breakdown of how this cost reduction was achieved:

### 1. Identifying the Leverage (The R-Theta Option)

The supply chain team, supported by engineering, identified a significantly cheaper alternative heatsink from a vendor named **R-Theta**.

- **The Competing Quote:** R-Theta quoted a heatsink price of **$3.61** (at 2,500 quantity) with a modest tooling charge of $425.
- **The Kontron Baseline:** Kontron, the supplier of the motherboard, was originally charging **$7.50** for their heatsink as part of a **$9.63** thermal/cable package.

### 2. The Strategic Pushback

We presented this alternative to Kontron, asking them to re-quote the motherboard assembly using the cheaper R-Theta part. This forced Kontron to defend their pricing model.

- **Kontron's Defense:** Kontron refused to use the third-party heatsink, stating that "unknown liabilities changing the design... will be better left with Kaleidescape." They argued that using a non-approved cooler would void the motherboard warranty because they could not guarantee correct "die pressure and cooling".

### 3. The Negotiation and Outcome

Despite Kontron's refusal to use the external part, the credible threat of us switching to a cheaper component forced them to sharpen their pencil to keep the business as a "single source" solution.

- **The Final Price:** Kontron dropped their package price from **$9.63 to $9.25**.
- **The Logic:** Rob Kuiper (Director of Supply Chain) accepted this reduction because it maintained Kontron as a "single source solution for both the board and heat sink," thereby eliminating the risk of warranty disputes if failures occurred.

### 4. Engineering Impact

While the final financial transaction was handled by Supply Chain, the negotiation was only possible because Engineering validated the alternative R-Theta part as a viable threat. I engineered the cost-reduction strategy by validating that a simpler, passive aluminum extrusion could meet the thermal requirements, which gave us the data needed to challenge the vendor's pricing.

## The "Thumb of God" Protocol

The "Thumb of God" failure was a catastrophic manufacturing defect discovered during the Orpheus pilot build at Sanmina, characterized by **mechanical violence** during assembly rather than a component failure. It earned its dramatic name because the physical force applied by assemblers (the "thumb") was inadvertently destroying the hardware.

### 1. The Mechanism of Failure

The "Player PCB" had a protruding section (referred to as a "peninsula") and was located in an electronics tray with extremely tight clearances (3-4mm). When assemblers slid the board into the chassis and tightened the screws, the lack of structural rigidity in the tray caused the board to flex. This flexing drove the board against the sharp metal edge of the tray, which would **shear off resistor R332** or fracture the solder joints of the BGA (Ball Grid Array) chips.

- **The Component Victim:** The sheared component, resistor R332, was located on the **Gennum JTAG port**. When this resistor was knocked off, it forced the video processor chip (Gennum) into a test mode.
- **The Symptom:** The units would fail to boot properly, entering a "Gennum could not talk to chip" crash loop. This resulted in a **20% fallout rate** on the assembly line, threatening to scrap the entire production run.
- **The Root Cause:** The design relied on the PCB to provide structural integrity to the carrier frame, rather than the frame supporting the PCB. The sheer density of cables required the board to be forced into the box, creating the stress that led to the fracture.

### 2. The Solution: Rigidifying the Assembly

Erik Norris determined that the board needed to be mechanically constrained to prevent this bowing. He coordinated with the PCB designer (Vince Di Lello) to add **four specific mounting holes** (referred to as locations 1, 6, 8, and 11) to the board and chassis assembly.

- **Locations 1 & 6:** These were added to the sides of the board to prevent it from bowing along the edges during handling and insertion.
- **Locations 8 & 11:** These were the most critical additions. They were positioned directly next to the **Gennum and TL chips** (the "two most sensitive BGA" components) to firmly secure the board area around them, preventing the flex that was fracturing the solder joints.

### 3. Implementation

The fix required a "re-spin" of the sheet metal to add mounting points and a revision of the PCB layout to clear traces for the new holes. The mechanical rigidification, combined with the "peninsula cut" on the tray (removing metal to clear a protruding section of the PCB), stabilized the production yield and eliminated the 20% fallout rate.
