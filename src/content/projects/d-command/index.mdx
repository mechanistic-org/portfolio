I. THE PHYSICS OF THE PROBLEM
Unlike the steel chassis of the D-Control ("Buckley"), the D-Command ("Danko") utilized injection-molded ABS plastic top covers to reduce cost and weight,. Plastic is transparent to electromagnetic radiation, creating a "Faraday Cage" deficit. To pass strict FCC Class A emissions standards, Norris engineered a composite shielding architecture.
II. THE ANATOMY OF THE SOLUTION
1. The "Skin" (Conductive Coating & Masking)
• The Strategy: Application of a conductive EMI coating (nickel/copper graphite paint) to the interior surfaces of the plastic covers to mimic a metal enclosure.
• The Crisis (Sticky Buttons): Early formulations of the coating were applied too thickly or with poor masking, causing the paint to build up inside the button holes. This caused the plastic buttons to bind and stick,.
• The Intervention: Norris qualified a "new formula" EMI coating and enforced strict masking protocols.
• The Validation: The approved coating measured < 4 ohms resistance corner-to-corner (high conductivity) while maintaining "excellent" masking that eliminated button friction,.
2. The "Bridge" (Copper Foil Tape)
• The Crisis: The conductive paint on the plastic needed a low-impedance path to the system ground (the metal chassis/PCBs).
• The Intervention (ECO 6531): Norris issued ECO 6531 to add Copper Foil EMI Shielding Tape (P/N 160209183-00) to the assembly BOMs. This tape bridged the conductive interior of the plastic covers to the grounding points on the metal sub-structure,.
• The Spec: The tape was applied to specific locations on the Fader Top, Main Top, and Main Bottom covers to ensure electrical continuity across the "skin".
3. The "Seal" (Gaskets & Interferences)
• The Crisis (The Missing Part): During the ramp to production, Manufacturing discovered a critical EMI Gasket was missing from the Bill of Materials (BOM). The original D-Control gasket worked if modified (cut), but a dedicated part was needed.
• The Intervention (ECO 8114): Norris executed a "Revised ECO" (8114) to release a specific EMI gasket (P/N 9440-13240-01) for D-Command. This gasket sealed the seam between the plastic top cover and the metal chassis to prevent RF leakage,,.
• The RJ45 Leak: Initial scans showed radiation leaking from the RJ45 connector area. Norris and the compliance team (Phong Do) modified cable routing and improved the gasket sealing around this port to suppress the noise.
4. The "Geometry" (The 80/20 Rule)
• The Philosophy: Norris applied "Design for Emissions" principles early in the CAD phase, specifically controlling maximum slot length in the plastic/metal interface.
• The Result: By limiting the physical dimensions of openings (slots) to be smaller than the wavelength of the expected emissions frequencies, the enclosure naturally attenuated radiation. Norris noted that despite having 3 distinct emitters and wireless protocols, the unit passed its first pre-scan with "zero modifications required" regarding the enclosure geometry itself.

Identity Anchor: "The Architect" (Erik Norris) Mandate: Engineer the D-Command ("Danko") console to meet global safety standards, enabling worldwide shipment. Core Achievement: Secured the UL Notice of Approval (NOA) on April 19, 2005, certifying the product for US, Canadian, and European markets just days before the first customer shipment on April 28.
II. THE SAFETY STANDARDS (The Rulebook)
To achieve this certification, the design adhered to a specific matrix of harmonized international safety standards.
1. The "Big Three" Safety Standards
The D-Command Main Unit and Fader Module were tested and certified to comply with the following specific standards:
• UL 60065 7th Edition: The primary US safety standard for audio, video, and similar electronic apparatus.
• IEC 60065 7th Edition: The International Electrotechnical Commission standard, ensuring global compatibility.
• CAN/CSA C22.2 No. 60065:03: The Canadian standard harmonized with the UL and IEC requirements.
2. Electromagnetic Compatibility (EMC)
In addition to physical safety, the device met stringent emissions and immunity standards:
• FCC Part 15 Class A: US standard for commercial equipment emissions.
• EN55103-1 (Emissions) & EN55103-2 (Immunity): European standards specifically for professional audio/video equipment in Environment E4.
• CISPR 22 Class A / AS/NZS 3548 Class A: International and Australian/New Zealand emissions standards.
III. THE ANATOMY OF COMPLIANCE (Forensic Evidence)
1. The AC Withdraw Failure (The Crisis)
The Trigger: During final testing, the D-Command initially failed the "AC Withdraw test" (UL 60065 subclause 9.1.6). The voltage on the mains plug pins did not drop below 60Vdc within 2 seconds of unplugging, posing a shock hazard. The Intervention:
• Resistor Modification: The engineering team identified that the 1M ohm bleeder resistor on the AC harness was insufficient. They recalculated and required a change to 750k ohm to drain the voltage faster.
• Component Swap: Investigated switching to a Delta AC Line filter (03DEEG3E-R) with a built-in bleeder resistor to eliminate the need for manual crimping on the harness.
2. Material Flammability (The "Tinderbox")
The Standard: All printed circuit boards (PCBs) and major plastic components were strictly specified as UL 94 V-0 (burning stops within 10 seconds on a vertical specimen). The Near-Miss: A forensic audit revealed that prototype PCBs from a specific vendor were marked UL 94 V-2, which was described by engineering as "a virtual tinderbox" compared to the required V-0. This triggered an immediate lockdown to ensure no V-2 boards ever reached production.
3. Thermal & Ventilation Mandates
The Data: The "Vegas Mode" stress test pushed internal heatsink temperatures to 61°C. The Compliance Fix: To maintain the safety rating of the power supplies (rated for 50°C ambient), the design mandated strict cable routing to prevent internal wiring from blocking airflow. The user manual explicitly warns: "Do not install D-Command anywhere or in any way that blocks free air flow at any time around the back panel".

III. THE FORENSIC FINDING
While the power supplies were rated for an ambient operating temperature of 50°C, the 61°C heatsink temperature indicated the component was operating at the thermal limit.
• The Risk: Internal cabling (the "snake") was identified as a potential thermal insulator. If cables were allowed to drape over the power supply, they would block the natural convection currents required to strip heat from the sink,.
• The "Win": The Meter Bridge remained "barely warm to the touch." This validated the cost-reduction decision to use single-color LEDs (Yellow) rather than the bi-color LEDs used on D-Control. The switch reduced the LED count by 25% and halved the number of driver chips, significantly lowering the thermal load on the top PCBA,.
