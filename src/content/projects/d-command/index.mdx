# D-Command: adapting a console architecture

D-Command, known internally as Danko, brought D-Control's control-surface architecture into a smaller console. My responsibility was lead mechanical engineering, including the part and assembly drawings, more than 24 unique sheet-metal parts and nine PCB dimensional-control drawings. Reuse reduced the amount of new hardware, but it left a substantial integration problem: inherited controls and boards had to meet new panels, a compact structure and a different manufacturing process. Electrical design, software, industrial design, sourcing, regulatory execution and production remained shared responsibilities. {/* ^[evidence:LK-7af43865] ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

## A compact main unit with room to expand

The main unit combined eight channel strips with transport, monitoring and dedicated processing controls. A separate 16-channel fader module made a 24-fader system. External analog monitoring connections went through XMON, a separate shared interface controlled from the console. That distinction matters to the mechanical account: packaging the D-Command controls and integrating their interfaces did not make the whole monitoring system my design. {/* ^[evidence:EV-914b078a3ebb] ^[evidence:EV-ac69fd5a210e] */}

The assembly drawings show the structure beneath the surface. Extruded rails and sheet-metal parts support the panel assemblies above a perforated pan. The main unit packages channel-strip hardware beside the larger control sections, with power supplies below. The boards, switches, knobs, panel openings and structural supports have to agree on where each interface sits. A PCB dimensional-control drawing defines that mechanical agreement; circuit and PCB-layout work remained with the electrical team. {/* ^[evidence:EV-914b078a3ebb] ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

## Reuse changed the machining and the interfaces

In June 2004, I sent extrusion prototype definitions that retained existing D-Control rails unchanged and reused other extrusion blanks with different secondary machining. The November parts list and December assembly trees show the same approach at another level: new D-Command structure and electronics alongside selected inherited board assemblies. Reuse was a part-by-part decision, not a uniform reduction of the larger console. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] ^[evidence:EV-914b078a3ebb] */}

One useful tradeoff concerned the transport-switch board. In May, Marc Schuman recorded the team's decision to mount it from below instead of proceeding with the earlier arrangement and its additional molded cover. I participated with Neal Breitbarth, Gannon Kashiwa, Bob Gardyne and Marc. The change required a new dimensional-control drawing and PCB layout, but no electrical changes. Avoiding extra tooling was a reason for the decision; the correspondence does not turn the estimate into a measured program saving. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

The inherited main-meter board offered a smaller example. Marc asked whether its unused lower extension could be removed for Danko without moving the mounting holes. I confirmed that there was no mechanical reason to keep it, and the team proceeded with the simpler outline. The mounting interface stayed stable while unneeded board area could be removed. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

The visible shape also received its own review. A September discussion preferred a smooth side-plastic treatment over a scaled version of Buckley's gills. Eli Manjarrez recorded the shared decision after meeting with Stan and me. The choice was one part of the team's industrial-design work. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

## A conductive skin that still let the buttons move

D-Command's molded ABS control panels replaced steel top covers used on D-Control. The plastic needed conductive coating to participate in the enclosure's shielding, while its many openings still had to clear switches and other controls. The panel drawings connect those concerns: material requirements, openings, bosses, masking and appearance surfaces all belong to the same part. {/* ^[evidence:LK-7af43865] ^[evidence:EV-914b078a3ebb] */}

Early coating buildup interfered with button movement. In December 2004, I reported receiving the three panel types with a revised EMI-coating formula, measuring less than 4 ohms corner-to-corner and finding no sticky buttons. I approved those samples for texturing. This was a specific sample disposition, not a claim that every production panel was defect-free. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

The coating was only one part of enclosure closure. ECO 6531 added copper foil to bridge the coated plastic to chassis ground; ECO 8114 addressed the missing EMI gasket. Routing and sealing also mattered near the RJ45 opening. The final system passed FCC Class A, CE and Canadian emissions. These were connected mechanical and electrical efforts, with the later gasket work still appearing in the post-ship cleanup list. {/* ^[evidence:LK-7af43865] ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

## Testing the fanless arrangement

Marc Schuman and Gannon Kashiwa ran the August 2004 thermal trial on the fader unit, which combined a smaller enclosure with greater dissipation than the main unit. The unit sat flat on a table, restricting the underside air entry. Marc estimated room temperature at 20-22°C. The record separates two operating conditions: {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

| Test condition | Internal “attic” | LED-supply heatsink |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Continuous fader motion in Vegas Mode, after roughly 30-40 minutes | 31°C | 56°C |
| Pro Tools operation with nearly all LEDs lit and the faders moving | 41°C | 61°C |

Marc and Eli explicitly called the results preliminary. They were useful evidence about the arrangement, not a substitute for complete qualification. A heatsink reading also cannot be subtracted from the supply's ambient rating to produce a temperature margin. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] ^[evidence:LK-7af43865] */}

The mechanical response preserved the natural-convection path. Manufacturing instructions kept the internal cable bundle away from the supplies, and the user guidance carried the airflow warning. The production method had to preserve the air space on which the fanless design depended. {/* ^[evidence:LK-7af43865] */}

## Drawings were an integration responsibility

By January 2005, the main and fader assembly drawings were circulating for review. In February, Neal described me as working solo on the part and assembly drawings while other team members helped with BOM and SAP work. Reviewers still needed time for redlines and access to the correct revisions. Concentrated drawing ownership existed inside a cross-functional release process. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

That responsibility linked the physical design to the build. The main-unit drawing carries the panel and board assemblies, rails, pan and supplies into one assembly definition. The plastic drawing carries changes through tooling, transport-switch webs and conductive treatment. A design change could require a corresponding drawing, BOM, board-interface or manufacturing update before the intended hardware reached the line. {/* ^[evidence:EV-914b078a3ebb] */}

## Compliance and the production handoff

The pre-production AC-withdrawal test found that unplugged mains-pin voltage did not fall below 60 Vdc within two seconds. Phong Do and Ken Lee worked with UL on the failure. The line-to-neutral bleeder changed from 1 MΩ to 750 kΩ; production later moved to a filter with an integral bleeder, removing the manual harness crimp. This was a regulatory and electrical correction that also affected the assembly process. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] ^[evidence:LK-7af43865] */}

UL issued its Notice of Approval on April 19, 2005. First customer ship followed on April 28, nine days later. That launch did not end mechanical work. The May cleanup list still tracked gasket, label, rear-bracket, side-plate and fit changes, and distinguished identified ECOs from changes that had completed approval. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] ^[evidence:LK-7af43865] */}

The same handoff separated remaining mechanical deliverables from further assembly improvements. Manufacturing would decide which of those improvements to pursue and implement, with me available as a consultant. The record establishes a transfer of responsibility, not that every suggestion was implemented or that May 13 was the exact end of my involvement. {/* ^[evidence:EV-b70eaf0a39b2] */}

D-Command's mechanical work extended from adapting existing parts to defining new interfaces and carrying them into production. The useful result is visible in the assembled product, but also in the decisions beneath it: preserve a mounting pattern, change a secondary machining operation, control a coating boundary, protect an airflow path, and make the released drawings agree with the build.
