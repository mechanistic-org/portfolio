## Carrying Argyle into a combination player

Cinema One brought a movie library and an optical-disc player into a single Kaleidescape enclosure. The brief was to carry the established Argyle design language into that combination product: a broad silver body, a curved white front, and a restrained set of visible controls. I developed the Cinema One surfaces and owned the mechanical design, engineering and product-development cycle. {/* ^[evidence:EV-50fd241c87ed] ^[evidence:LK-8c67248d] */}

That responsibility connected the external form to the parts and processes needed to build it. The front had to accommodate the disc opening, buttons, illuminated graphics and infrared receiver. Behind it were a molded frame, metal chassis, optical drive, hard drive, fan and electronics. The challenge was to make those interfaces fit within an exterior intended to look simple. {/* ^[evidence:EV-50fd241c87ed] ^[evidence:LK-8ef62a80] */}

## A molded cover with a metal structure

The silver outer body was an injection-molded plastic cover reinforced with separate metal stiffeners. The cover assembly drawing shows a top stiffener, left and right stiffeners, and a rear shield fitted inside the molded shell. Pressure-sensitive adhesive joined the cover and its stiffeners. This construction let the exterior form and the internal sheet-metal structure be developed as connected parts of the same assembly. {/* ^[evidence:LK-36bc04f0] ^[evidence:LK-8ef62a80] */}

The front was another assembly in its own right. A plastic bezel frame carried the decorated faceplate, button components and IR lens. The May 2013 bill of materials identifies Yomura for the molded cover, Mass Precision for the metalwork and Nissha for the decorated faceplate. Their parts had to meet at the visible edges while accommodating the drive and electronics behind them. {/* ^[evidence:LK-8ef62a80] */}

The assembly instructions make the relationships tangible. They separate the drive, fan, bezel, cover and chassis work, then bring the subassemblies together. One fixture locates the front assembly against the cover; a later step positions the front bezel at 90 degrees to the chassis so its ribbon cable can be connected. Assembly access was part of the geometry problem. {/* ^[evidence:LK-9b3723bf] */}

## Working through the interfaces

A March 2013 review from Function Engineering examined the tolerance loops between the bezel, cover, disc slot, sheet metal, alignment features and switches. Its bezel-to-cover example compared a nominal 0.5 mm gap with a calculated root-sum-square variation of ±0.92 mm under the initial assumptions. That made the risk explicit: the intended reveal depended on more than nominal CAD alignment. {/* ^[evidence:LK-0f61ade9] */}

The review considered increasing the gap, tightening molded-part tolerances and changing how the parts were located during assembly. A companion calculation using tighter tolerances reduced the statistical estimate, but its worst-case variation still exceeded the nominal gap. The report also required vendors to confirm the assumed process tolerances. These were tools for choosing geometry and assembly methods, with manufacturing capability still to establish. {/* ^[evidence:LK-0f61ade9] ^[evidence:LK-341ee05d] */}

Other interfaces brought different constraints. The disc had unequal clearances above and below the front opening. Alignment pins had limited lead-in, and feet could change the relationship between parts during cover installation. The review considered centering the disc opening, changing lead-ins and using assembly spacers. Small clearances affected both the finished appearance and the sequence by which the enclosure could close. {/* ^[evidence:LK-0f61ade9] ^[evidence:LK-d54010d6] */}

## Revising the parts and the build

The April and May 2013 plastic-change records show the work moving through individual features: extending the cover's bezel shroud, transferring a slot rib to the front frame, adjusting button contact geometry, adding screw holes, and providing clearance for gate vestiges and ejector mismatch. Changes to the cover, frame, button tree and faceplate had to stay coordinated because they met at the same front assembly. {/* ^[evidence:LK-02d7d7d5] ^[evidence:LK-b3d05959] */}

The IR lens provides a compact example of that continued refinement. Its drawing records the addition of an internal bounce rib on June 9, 2013, followed by relocation of the rib on August 7. The geometry changed within the existing lens and front assembly. The drawing establishes those revisions; it does not supply a measured remote-control range result. {/* ^[evidence:LK-c180c650] */}

Supplier feedback also dealt with what happened after molding. In the 2013 cover dossier, a marked photograph questions whether flash removal had chamfered an edge that needed to remain sharp. Yomura's annotations describe difficult manual trimming and, on October 25, report a sample processed by CNC milling. The exchange made the desired edge condition visible and put a specific alternative in front of the team for review. {/* ^[evidence:LK-4d1bf860] */}

The dated record runs from prototype drawing releases in October 2012 through production drawing releases and Cinema One's July 2013 introduction. Assembly-document revisions continued through that year, covering fasteners, torque instructions and EMI-gasket placement. Mechanical development included this ongoing work of turning released geometry into a repeatable build. {/* ^[evidence:LK-36bc04f0] ^[evidence:LK-8c67248d] ^[evidence:LK-9b3723bf] */}

## Making cosmetic judgments inspectable

In October 2014, top-cover appearance required another kind of engineering work. I cut ten covers to obtain a straight camera view of the bend surface, keeping the surface parallel to the sensor because the depth of field was narrow. I photographed a range of defects and asked colleagues to vote on numbered pass/fail examples. The purpose was to make the acceptance discussion specific enough to communicate to the factory. {/* ^[evidence:EV-f8cf01c939d0] */}

Mark McKenzie, Nathan Forster and Rob Wudrick participated in the review. I reordered and renumbered the examples, then asked whether the document could be released to Sanmina. Mark approved that release. This photographic acceptance work followed the earlier sample and trimming exchanges; it addressed how to judge existing parts under defined viewing and cosmetic criteria. {/* ^[evidence:EV-181cc9638f36] ^[evidence:LK-a97ac9c7] */}

The manufacturing record still showed parts awaiting further inspection. I do not treat a revised inspection rule as proof that molding quality improved or that the production shortage was resolved. The contribution documented here is the connection from exterior surfaces to detailed mechanical parts, supplier revisions, assembly methods and an explicit visual basis for making quality decisions. {/* ^[evidence:EV-8d88339190f6] ^[evidence:EV-50fd241c87ed] */}
