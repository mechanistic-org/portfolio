# D-Control: making a modular console buildable

D-Control, known internally as Buckley, put a large Pro Tools control surface on a configurable stand. I designed the stand and took over Matt Cho's mechanical work early in the program. The responsibility grew into console integration, manufacturing recovery and sustaining changes. Matt's earlier work remains part of that history: the surviving 2002-2003 PCB interface drawings carry his authorship. Electrical design, software, industrial design, purchasing, quality and production were shared team responsibilities. {/* ^[evidence:EV-39ce32cd952d] ^[evidence:LK-4881fb2e] ^[evidence:EV-951ecdcc7a79] */}

## One console, several configurations

The product combined a main unit with separate 16-channel fader modules. The D-Control guide describes expansion to 80 faders: five fader modules **plus the main unit**. Each main or fader unit had its own power connection. That modularity made the stand and the interfaces between neighboring units central engineering problems. A configuration had to work as an assembled structure, with the surface controls, meter bridges and cosmetic edges meeting correctly. {/* ^[evidence:LK-4ba922ee] */}

The main-unit assembly drawing shows the physical layers: a lower pan, side structure and rails, internal supports, and upper control-panel assemblies. PCB dimensional-control drawings located board outlines, mounting holes, controls and connectors within that package. They defined mechanical interfaces to the electronics; they were not circuit-design ownership. {/* ^[evidence:LK-c27b2b7d] ^[evidence:LK-4881fb2e] ^[evidence:EV-e03cb2a04d81] */}

## The stand began with feature tradeoffs

The 2003 correspondence follows the stand before the production problems. In April I extended the heel of the foot cover to support the rear steel foot when the shelf was loaded, departing from the earlier industrial-design cutout. By May, unresolved price points were holding up the stand and shelving work. The June feature review put construction choices in front of the team: a separate bolt-on heel, a thick flat foot with a formed saddle, two-piece legs, shelf fasteners and cable routing. {/* ^[evidence:EV-4994d9c09406] */}

Those were interacting choices. A cable path inside the leg changed fabrication work; concealed shelf fasteners affected the shelf structure; the heel had to support the loaded arrangement while fitting the intended appearance. The June exchange records proposals and cost questions, rather than a single frozen design. The later stand packet carries those interfaces into part and assembly definitions. A July 6, 2004 drawing, for example, releases a stiffened main foot plate with a welded reinforcement under ECO 6325. {/* ^[evidence:EV-4994d9c09406] ^[evidence:LK-aff1c2ec] ^[evidence:EV-e03cb2a04d81] */}

Configuration work continued after the initial release. In August 2004, Tom Oiwa questioned the C-Mid and D-Mid crossbar lengths and the precision of their dimensions. I explained that the crossbars were intentionally shorter than the overall dimensions of the units they supported. The exchange is useful evidence of design intent and manufacturing review. Its four-decimal calculations do not establish a measured system tolerance. {/* ^[evidence:EV-4994d9c09406] */}

## Geometry had to survive assembly

The manufacturing instructions make alignment part of the build sequence. Both main and fader assemblies started with screws loose enough for adjustment. Before installing the covers, the process called for flatness and squareness checks across the top, front and inside surfaces. Assemblers were to loosen, realign and recheck a unit that failed those checks. The main-unit instructions also controlled harness routing and delayed the cosmetic overlays until after burn-in. {/* ^[evidence:LK-c27b2b7d] ^[evidence:EV-e03cb2a04d81] */}

The installation guide used the same principle at system scale. Alignment pins joined neighboring units while their attachments to the rails remained loose. A ratcheting nylon strap closed the stand legs; if necessary, it also brought the units together before the remaining fasteners were tightened. The guide explicitly kept the strap clear of switches and encoders. Adjustment was designed into the assembly method. {/* ^[evidence:LK-4ba922ee] */}

Inspection then tested whether the parts and process delivered that intent. In July 2004, Tom collected gap data from ten main and ten fader units to help define an allowable manufacturing gap. The following discussions still called for squareness checks with units both separate and installed, before ordering further fixtures. The gap and stand photographs show the interfaces under review; they do not prove that every photographed condition was resolved by one change. {/* ^[evidence:EV-4994d9c09406] */}

## Recovering the manufacturing process

The first 42 steel fader pans exposed a basic forming problem. PEM hardware insertion stretched the sheet locally and bowed the pans enough that more than half were rejected; the side-plate interfaces would not line up. My immediate response was to demonstrate cold work over a table edge to recover flatness. The supplier correction redistributed the insertion sequence and added flattening after insertion. Immediate salvage and the process change were separate parts of the recovery. {/* ^[evidence:LK-e6ac858e] */}

The June 2004 manufacturing and DFM campaign was estimated at two months; my retained account records completing it in 2.5 weeks, including home-built assembly jigs. The immediate recovery got usable parts and tooling into the process, while fit and production work continued afterward. {/* ^[evidence:LK-e6ac858e] */}

### Prototype board containment

A separate prototype-board investigation found V-2 material in 90% of one unnamed vendor's prototype population, where V-0 was required. Four shop logos exposed uncontrolled subcontracting. We quarantined the affected prototypes, checked customer exposure and terminated that vendor for prototype work. The retained correction record states that no affected boards shipped. This was a bounded containment result, separate from the pan and stand work. {/* ^[evidence:LK-e6ac858e] */}

## Sustaining work after launch

The engineering responsibility continued into installed systems and production changes. In June 2004 I volunteered as the mechanical group's point person for assembly and installation issues, working with installers, QA and manufacturing. The 2005 correspondence shows why that role still mattered: a successful part test did not automatically settle rework, documentation or release. {/* ^[evidence:EV-951ecdcc7a79] */}

For the replacement side plates, Brandon Cammack reported an aggressive manual test on February 15, 2005. He loaded and moved the side cap while driving the screw, damaging the cap while the fastening interface continued to hold. I approved the parts after that report. It was a useful abuse test, not a quantified qualification of every production unit. {/* ^[evidence:EV-951ecdcc7a79] */}

Getting the change into manufacturing took further work. The team still needed second-operation drawings and an ECO; the supplier's first rework setup scrapped parts. By April, QA reported acceptable reworked parts. Tom initiated urgent ECO 8045 to replace the old side plates with my 9420-13080-01/-02 designs, and I delivered the drawings and models on April 15. Purchasing, QA, manufacturing and mechanical engineering each owned part of that transition. {/* ^[evidence:EV-951ecdcc7a79] */}

The Swedish Television installation raised a different interface question. In April 2005 I reviewed the stand-flange condition and explained that the flange was intended to lie parallel to the underside of the unit. The concerning case was a flange sitting too high and preventing the pan from seating squarely on the rails. The selected correspondence establishes that diagnosis; it does not establish a completed replacement or inspection-gauge rollout. {/* ^[evidence:EV-951ecdcc7a79] */}

The plastic changes had their own verification trail. ECO 6310 enlarged mounting holes from 0.200 to 0.250 inch, enlarged their bosses and raised five bosses by 0.050 inch. Deviation 1212 covered interim reaming; the Rev B part record specifies Noryl. On August 1, 2005, I confirmed that the T1 samples incorporated the ECO changes and sent the signed approval to PPI, the molder. That is a documented sample-approval endpoint, not proof that all field fit issues had disappeared. ECO 8197's side-plate relief remains a separate change. {/* ^[evidence:LK-aff1c2ec] ^[evidence:LK-e6ac858e] ^[evidence:EV-4994d9c09406] */}

D-Control shipped and appears in the installed-system archive. The engineering outcome was a modular product supported by detailed part definitions, an adjustable assembly sequence, containment when parts failed and continued production support. The stand, console and cosmetic interfaces had to work together in the factory and again when assembled at a customer's studio. {/* ^[evidence:LK-e6ac858e] ^[evidence:LK-4ba922ee] ^[evidence:EV-951ecdcc7a79] */}
