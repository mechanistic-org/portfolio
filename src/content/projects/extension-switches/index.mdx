# Extension Switch (Elvis)

Noon's Extension Switch gave each group of lights a familiar physical control while communicating with the Room Director to coordinate the room. A click switched an individual light on or off; the narrow dimmer control adjusted its brightness. The product's simplicity at the wall depended on a more involved mechanism behind the cap.

My contribution was on the Locoroll mechanical-development and reliability side: reviewing molded parts and tooling, working through test requirements and following manufacturing issues with the suppliers. Colin Davis at Function Engineering authored the Elvis preload and housing-clearance analyses. FIH coordinated assembly processes and testing, while Memtech handled molding and tooling actions.

## A familiar switch with a coupled mechanism

The front cap, dimmer key, front housing and rear housing had to move and assemble together without losing the clean exterior. The assembly record shows the dimmer key and cap joined to the front case, with the circuit board, flex, carrier and spring assemblies behind them. This made cap retention, operating feel and the visible gaps related engineering problems.

## Holding the cap meant controlling the process

Cap separation appeared in ordinary handling in January 2017. The working issue record describes dirt collecting in the seam and parts separating easily. Increasing the tape area and selecting a stronger pressure-sensitive adhesive were early responses, but the revised tape still failed a March crush test.

The investigation also found a geometry mismatch. The adhesive pocket differed by 0.1 mm from the intended geometry, which translated into an uneven visible gap. The working synopsis records a tooling correction and revised samples received around April 20. Bonding material and joint geometry therefore had to be evaluated together.

FIH then tested glued samples. The working synopsis records drop and crush passes while asking how the new fixtures would control and inspect the gap after gluing. The May action review keeps the Elvis cap issue under DVT monitoring and identifies Ellen at FIH with the assembly-process work. Moving from successful samples to a repeatable operation required dispensing, positioning and inspection controls.

By the May 10 assembly SOP, the cap operation specified FC1682 glue, a dispensing fixture, a separate cap-placement fixture and at least five hours in a clamping jig. The accompanying material data sheet identifies FC-1682 as a moisture-reactive polyurethane. The manufacturing change was more than replacing tape with a stronger bond: it added dispensing, positioning and clamping controls around the joint.

## The feel depended on the hidden stack

A second problem sat behind the moving face. Colin Davis's tact-switch analysis followed the tolerance loop through the carrier, housings, shoulder screw, O-ring, circuit board and switch. Its conclusion warned that the preload could approach the actuation stroke and called for better understanding of insert control and O-ring compression. A separate analysis examined the clearance that allowed the front housing to tilt inside the rear housing.

The supplier action list connects that sensitivity to real assembly variation. Tilted screw nuts caused a key-feel problem; Memtech was tuning its heat-staking support and planning checks of nut alignment. Elsewhere, a main-switch squeak was traced to scratched spring-contact surfaces on units missing dry lubricant. The recorded response combined further life cycling with an instruction to retain the lubricant and add a UV-lamp inspection station.

## Test requirements and test results had different jobs

The reliability program was also being revised during development. In the March discussion recorded in the action review, FIH said the initial architecture had not been established around a ten-year life requirement and raised component-guarantee and schedule concerns. My feedback was part of the subsequent test-plan discussion. Turning that requirement into tests was an engineering task of its own.

The July key-life report retained an overall failure even while recording functional and visual passes. Its tactile measurements were judged against separate criteria. Later DVT summaries recorded a suggested change to tactile thresholds and waiver remarks alongside passed tests, failures and continuing work. Test results and acceptance decisions had to be tracked separately.

## Turning findings into inspectable parts

The exterior finish also needed concrete feedback. In my April 26 review of the dimmer-key steel polish, I documented uneven polish, stress marks and dents by mold cavity. The purpose was to turn a general cosmetic concern into defects a supplier could locate and correct in the tool.

The shared Sativa and Elvis materials work also kept alternatives open. A March proposal set out how to compare uncoated resin candidates with the existing coated material, using UV exposure, scratch testing and controlled photographic inspection before choosing a final material. It defined a selection process rather than recording a completed material change.

That work also depended on usable specifications and release information. The March working notes track gaps in cosmetic criteria, gap specifications, appearance callouts and drawing formats, along with requests to the people responsible for them. This documentation work connected design review to the measurements and acceptance decisions needed during a build.

## What the work established

The result was a defined cap-bonding operation, targeted tooling feedback and clearer checks for assembly feel and appearance. My contribution connected mechanical review and reliability questions to supplier actions and inspectable requirements, tying the visible behavior of the switch to the joint, part or process that controlled it.
