# M700 Disc Vault

The M700 made a large physical disc collection behave like a digital library. Its carousel stored up to 320 Blu-ray discs, DVDs and CDs, imported them automatically, and retrieved a selected disc through the front slot. Launched in July 2011, the five-rack-unit product also contained an M-Class player. That integrated player distinguishes M700 from the separate DV700 vault.

My work crossed the internal mechanical design, supplier and tooling reviews, product integration, and later reliability investigations. Function Engineering developed the early carousel, kicker and pinch-roller breadboards. Inside Kaleidescape, mechanical work had to meet the electronics, sensing and control work of the wider hardware team. The later sustaining effort included mechanisms shared with DV700, while the two products retained separate identities and return histories.

## Moving a disc through the machine

A disc entered between a pair of pinch rollers, moved into a carousel slot, and later transferred to the optical drive. The carousel, kicker and rollers formed one transport system. The disc changed height as it left its slot, so the rollers needed controlled vertical travel as well as grip. Constraining a disc too early would make the rollers skid; leaving its path poorly guided would let it miss the next opening.

Function Engineering's August 2010 report records the alternatives tested on the breadboard. Four slot geometries were evaluated; a V-groove was needed to constrain and align the disc with the kicker. The team compared timing-belt arrangements and selected an off-the-shelf MXL combination that gave an integral number of motor steps per slot. The pinch-roller assembly demonstrated six millimeters of vertical travel, refined ingress and egress guides, and a gearmotor isolation scheme to reduce transmitted noise.

Those results established feasibility in the breadboard. The report still called for component life testing, confirmation of sensing and positioning, complete-system handling tests, and development of the disc-retention cover. Reliable production required those separate pieces to work together.

## Turning the mechanism into a product

The project schedule assigned me internal mechanical design and release, prototype evaluation, production-tooling release and first-article review. The work included the enclosure and mounting parts around Function Engineering's mechanisms, plus the physical interfaces to Kaleidescape's boards and sensors. Nathan Forster's assignments included the front illumination and interface electronics; the schedule kept firmware and motor-control electronics under their respective engineering owners.

The details were coupled. My running design notes track removable inner guides for service access, operating clearance around the carousel, optical-drive brackets, common fasteners, wire routing and the position of the front sensor boards. They also record smaller assembly lessons. A 2011 inspection found fibers in the grease and changed the proposed applicator from cotton swabs to an acid brush. A seemingly minor process detail could put contamination beside a moving disc.

Production preparation involved reviewing real parts, identifying revisions and coordinating release. The April 2011 punch list records my release instructions alongside supplier lead times, samples awaiting approval, and tooling questions. It was a working boundary between design intent, the parts available for a build, and what still needed correction.

## A carousel that had to fill, release and stay flat

The large molded carousel combined hundreds of narrow disc slots with the belt and encoder interfaces around its perimeter. Its manufacturing problem was also a transport problem: irregular slot faces, flash or warpage could change the resistance encountered by a disc.

The January 2011 T2 review recorded a molding trial that produced no usable parts. Material stuck on both sides of the tool, and the operating window was too narrow. I recorded the review with Function Engineering and molding specialist Loyd May, including the next actions for the suppliers. The team considered feeder ribs, gate balance, cooling, venting and material behavior. Loyd recommended understanding venting and equalizing mold temperature before expecting a material change to solve the problem.

A later recommendation specified additional vents on 45 ejector pins. The October 2011 modification study also explored adding 320 sister ribs. These records show a sequence of proposed tooling interventions. They do not, by themselves, establish that every proposal was implemented or that one change resolved all later failures.

Flatness remained a practical inspection issue in 2014. An April review of a box of ten carousels found the bottom three more warped and questioned whether tight packaging trays were loading the parts. I was assigned to relax the packaging fit. The April 10 revision of the inspection procedure made the disposition explicit: locate the high points on a flat surface, measure their deflection, and reject and quarantine parts exceeding two millimeters.

The shared M700/DV700 carousel correspondence carried that inspection into supplier decisions. John Lio identified me as the engineer responsible for the carousel part and its disposition. On August 19, I instructed Sanmina to scrap the failed parts. The correspondence records that instruction and the supplier's acknowledgment; it does not record completion of the scrap work.

## More grip changed the alignment problem

Field investigations exposed a tradeoff in the roller assembly. Contamination reduced traction; more spring force could improve tolerance to dirty rollers and tight carousel slots. But increasing the force also bent the roller shafts. The interface needed stiffness as well as clamping load.

Nathan's March 2014 report describes two vaults in which discs ejected toward the clockwise side of the carousel and struck the slot rib. It connected that behavior to shaft bending and misalignment with the optical drive. My next work was to support the driven shaft through the inner guide and replace compliant motor-plate mounts with hard spacers. The April hardware review lists solid motor-plate mounts, an extended-neck gear, guide variants, a pillow-block extension and three spring versions among the designs I was preparing.

The guide also had to separate discs that clung together. Nathan tested moving it closer to the disc; I flagged the small clearance and prepared SLA versions to test. These changes addressed different failure paths. More roller force alone did not resolve double-disc handling, sensor behavior or drive alignment.

By April 29, I had loaded revised inner-guide and bearing files into the working revision in Arena. Fifty spring prototypes were on order. I called for testing and confirmation before loading the springs; Rob Wudrick proposed releasing the plastic parts independently and bringing the changes into the assembly bill of materials together.

The disc-retention cover was another alignment interface. In May, John Lio circulated assembly instructions for both M700 and DV700: use the enlarged mounting holes with washers, leave the roller brackets in place, align the cover without loading the outer screws, and tighten the center and perimeter fasteners in sequence. The instruction addressed distortion introduced during assembly.

Those shared M700/DV700 changes progressed beyond the April prototypes. In July, I loaded the spring file into Arena and said it could be released with the revised guide and bearing. My August 4 status report recorded preparation for engineering-change release and approval to purchase spring tooling. Assembly-instruction updates and further evaluation remained on the work list.

## Testing what the complaint actually meant

The investigation depended on separating a reported symptom from a reproduced mechanism. The roller test record documents returned assemblies installed in a known-good unit, followed by repeated front and rear transfers. Some long runs failed to reproduce the original complaint while still revealing disc scratching, alignment details or contamination. Passing one transfer test did not clear every physical issue.

The shared test setup itself also needed scrutiny. I initially attributed rear-roller binding to the disc-retention cover. In February, I found that a longer screw I had installed in the lower sensor-board mount pushed the optical drive out of alignment. Replacing it with the correct shorter screw restored injection and ejection in that setup. I withdrew the cover diagnosis.

Software and operating conditions mattered too. March correspondence describes reversed discs, matte labels that changed sensor behavior, insufficient roller run time during slippage, and drive-region settings among the paths being investigated. Those belonged to a shared hardware, firmware and service effort, with different failures requiring different responses.

In March 2014, Mark McKenzie's field-data review gave the team a wider view: about three million inject operations across the vault population, excluding manufacturing. The analysis compared weekly changes for individual units and retained gaps left by missed collection periods in 2012. Mark also corrected his earlier interpretation of which failure category affected more units. The value lay in understanding the population and the failure definitions together.

## What the work established

M700 reached the market as a 320-disc vault with an integrated player. The later engineering record shows a sustained effort to make that transport more tolerant of real discs, contamination, variation and assembly. My contribution connected mechanical definition, suppliers, inspection and corrective designs to the wider team's continuing investigation.

The work produced defined inspection rules, revised guide and bearing files, preparation for spring-change release, tooling purchase approval and a disposition instruction for failed carousels. Testing and assembly updates remained part of the continuing work. The selected records do not establish a completed fleet retrofit or a measured reduction in field failures.
